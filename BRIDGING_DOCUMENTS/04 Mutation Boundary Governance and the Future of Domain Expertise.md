# Mutation Boundary Governance and the Future of Domain Expertise

## 1. The Displacement Frame

The dominant public frame for AI's relationship to professional labor is displacement. Every task an AI can do is a task a human used to do; every capability gain is a labor contraction. Practitioners reading this paper will have encountered some version of this frame, often repeatedly, and may bring its assumptions with them. Engaging seriously with their concerns is the right starting point. Dismissing the frame would be unserious, and would invite the dismissal the framework most needs to avoid: the assumption that AI advocates do not understand the labor stakes of what they are proposing.

The frame is not invented. It tracks a real concern, and it has produced a substantial body of analysis — automation forecasts, occupational impact studies, transition policy proposals — that takes the zero-sum dynamic seriously. Some of that analysis is well-reasoned, and some of it is alarmist; but neither extreme should obscure the reasonable underlying point. Some tasks within professional work will be reduced or eliminated by AI capability gains. Document review at scale. Certain forms of pattern recognition. Certain intermediate analytical products. Certain administrative workflows that currently consume professional time without requiring professional judgment. Practitioners who deny that any displacement will occur are not engaging seriously with the actual capability landscape, and the framework should not ask them to.

The frame is incomplete, however, in a specific way. It treats AI mainly as a substitute for human capability: a system that does work people previously did, faster or cheaper. For some tasks, this is correct. But where opaque generators affect durable consequential state, an additional category of work appears: defining, testing, supervising, and maintaining the rules under which their proposals become commitments. That work requires legitimate domain authority; model fluency alone cannot supply the field's lawful ontology, evidentiary criteria, and permissions. This is an argument that AI participation creates *new requirements for expert governance*, not proof that every incumbent professional position survives or that total employment rises. The question of how much work those requirements support, given reuse and automation, must be examined separately.

The paper does not deny displacement. Its positive claim is that mutation-boundary governance makes visible categories of professional work that substitution-only forecasts can overlook: constitutional authorship, domain-local adaptation, operational adjudication where required, maintenance, assurance, and negotiation across fields. Whether these outweigh labor displaced elsewhere is an empirical economic question. The structural case for legitimate human authority can hold even if some deployments reuse a square at scale and employ fewer people for routine tasks.

Two objections deserve explicit treatment. First, **reuse**: if a square can be authored once and deployed repeatedly, why would many experts be needed rather than a small constitutional committee? Second, **apprenticeship**: many of the proposer-level tasks AI can displace — initial case reviews, drafts, analyses — are also how novices have historically acquired the judgment needed to become experts. The same framework that needs legitimate authority may erode a pathway for producing its successors. Neither objection is answered merely by identifying the founder role. Section 5 addresses the scale of expert work across deployment stages and the supervised formation of future authorities.

## 2. Institutional Continuity

Every institution humans have built for governing consequential action across opaque agents — law, contract, peer review, parliamentary procedure, scientific publication, medical boards, professional licensure, accreditation, regulatory oversight — operates at the boundary between agents whose interiors are mutually inaccessible. A judge cannot inspect the interior reasoning of an advocate. A reviewer cannot inspect the interior process by which a researcher reached a conclusion. A medical board cannot inspect the diagnostic reasoning that produced a clinical decision. What these institutions can do is govern the boundary: require that arguments be presented in canonical form, audit transitions between provisional and committed claims, hold authorities accountable for their commitments, provide review mechanisms for retrospective correction. These are not imperfect substitutes for direct inspection of interiors. They are the correct site of governance between systems whose internal manifolds are opaque to each other, and they have been refined through centuries of institutional practice.

The Mutation Boundary Problem is the same problem applied to a new kind of agent. An LLM is opaque. Its interior is not accessible to outside observers in the way governance requires. Its outputs may become consequential in shared domains where lawful structure must be honored. The governance pattern that works for opaque human agents — render the boundary lawful, audit transitions, hold authority accountable — applies to opaque machine agents as well. The substrate is new. The pattern is ancient.

This means the corpus does not ask practitioners to become AI researchers before they can contribute lawful domain judgment. It does ask them to learn enough about qualification, evidence, and operational limits to understand where their authority is exercised, with technical collaborators where necessary. A physician familiar with medical boards already knows why consequential decisions require accountable standards despite incomplete access to a colleague's interior reasoning. Extending that discipline to AI is continuous with clinical governance, but rendering it as a tested, model-facing constitution adds obligations that professional intuition cannot silently fulfill.

The same continuity holds across professions. Attorneys operate within an institutional form — bar admission, professional responsibility rules, judicial review, appellate process — whose entire structure is mutation-boundary governance applied to legal reasoning. The court system does not inspect the interior of an advocate's mind; it governs the boundary at which legal claims become committed positions, requires those claims to be presented in canonical form, and provides review mechanisms when commitments later prove to have been wrongly made. Scientists operate within peer review and publication, which is mutation-boundary governance applied to scientific claims. The journal does not inspect the interior of a researcher's reasoning; it governs the boundary at which provisional findings become published claims that the field treats as part of the durable record. Researchers operate within IRB review and grant oversight, which is mutation-boundary governance applied to research conduct itself. Each profession's existing institutional form is a partial template for what AI participation in that profession requires.

What the corpus contributes is a pattern practitioners may adapt through existing professional institutions rather than necessarily constructing every governance process anew. Constitutional founding extends familiar expert responsibilities — identifying legitimate authority, evidence standards, and lawful transitions — into machine-legible form. Medical boards, peer review panels, bar committees, and institutional review bodies provide partial precedents. Their existing mechanisms may accommodate some square governance, but federation, independent technical assurance, and cross-domain admissibility work could require new bodies or arrangements. Institutional continuity is a starting resource, not a guarantee of organizational sufficiency.

A limit of institutional continuity belongs here because Section 5 depends on it. Existing boards, review committees, and professional societies furnish precedents for oversight of established authorities, but they do not fully solve how governance rules are rendered, qualified on actual AI substrates, or staffed across independent organizations. Nor can they by themselves guarantee the practical training through which novices acquire judgment. Continuity helps answer how experienced authorities might begin this work; it does not *completely* answer that question, and it leaves the supply of future authorities as the separate apprenticeship problem Section 5 examines.

## 3. The Constitutional-Founder Role

The corpus's commit-authority requirement places domain experts at the center of any legitimate square. Without a domain expert holding commit authority over their field's lawful structure, a square cannot exist. With one, the square can be produced through the disciplined collaboration the bridging document on producing a square develops in detail.

This role is what the paper calls constitutional founder. The domain expert is the authority who renders their field's lawful structure into machine-legible form, who governs that rendering's compliance with the rectangle's invariants, and who maintains commit authority over what the architecture actually does in their domain. The role is constitutional in the strict sense: the founder defines what counts as lawful within their domain, what authorities operate within it, what evidence governs change, what transitions are permitted. These are constitutional questions in any field, and AI participation does not change who has standing to answer them. They remain the practitioners' questions. The framework does not answer them; it provides the form in which the practitioners' answers can be rendered to govern AI participation.

The role is continuous with existing professional roles, with one new dimension. A physician who already holds clinical authority adds the dimension of rendering some aspect of that authority into a form that governs AI participation in clinical practice. An attorney who already holds professional authority adds the dimension of rendering some aspect of that authority into a form that governs AI participation in legal practice. A researcher who already holds research authority adds the dimension of rendering research-conduct standards into a form that governs AI participation in research. The expertise transfers. The discipline transfers. What is added is the rendering work and the maintenance work that follows from it.

The role does not require every constitutional founder to become an ML engineer or transformer specialist. The [method demonstration](./02%20Producing%20a%20Square%20A%20Method%20Demonstration.md) shows how LLMs can propose auxiliary technical and architectural content while the practitioner judges it against the domain's lawful structure. But this division of labor does not waive substrate qualification, audit requirements, or the need for technical collaborators where implementation demands them. A physician rendering a clinical decision boundary into machine-legible form is doing work related to articulating guidelines or teaching residents, with an added engineering obligation: the rules must be inspectable, the model roles qualified, and the proper-use boundary demonstrably respected rather than assumed from the founder's comprehension.

The role also has an institutional dimension at scale. Professional societies, standards committees, boards, and peer-review bodies can provide existing venues for negotiating domain rules and coordinating squares; some institutions may extend those venues without creating separate organizations. Other needs — independent cross-domain admission, common technical assurance, or enduring federation agreements — may warrant new institutional forms. OTTC specifies functional governance requirements, not the organizational chart that must satisfy them. Practitioners can begin with structures they know while allowing the institutional design to evolve where the boundary work genuinely requires it.

## 4. What Careers Look Like

In medicine, a clinician might help specify the authorized inputs, evidence thresholds, transition rules, and escalation authority for a narrowly scoped workflow; qualified implementation and local oversight would still be needed, and clinical responsibility would not automatically move to a model. Such rendering work may become a specialization or a portion of a clinician's practice. It does not follow that every existing task, staffing level, or clinical workflow would remain unchanged.

In law, an attorney might render a particular document-handling or procedural workflow, specifying whose assertions count as evidence, what privilege or disclosure rules control transfer, and which conclusions require authorized legal judgment. The resulting square would have to be maintained as jurisdiction and practice change. This is a governance function for qualified professionals, not a prediction that automation leaves document review, drafting, or staffing untouched.

In research, a scientist might help govern provenance, preregistration, evidentiary status, and the conditions for correcting or withdrawing a standing hypothesis. Multi-domain Hypothesis Machines are a longer-term possibility, not a present employment market. Near-term value would lie in the specified standards and their inspection by people with standing to evaluate the science.

In education, a practitioner might govern the difference between an answer that sounds correct and evidence that a learner can apply a concept independently. A square could declare when assessment results warrant advancement, remediation, or another form of instruction under the institution's lawful standards. Teaching, assessment, and instructional supervision still require judgment; which activities are automated, expanded, or retained will vary by institution and deployment.

The educator case also illustrates why consequences differ by domain. Educational errors may be recoverable through revised instruction or reassessment, but that cannot be assumed for every student or decision. The proper-use boundary, scope of automation, evidence obligations, and review cadence must be set by the domain's legitimate authority rather than imported wholesale from a clinical, legal, or narrative square.

A deliberately small non-narrative rendering example makes the founder's work visible. A university laboratory's *hypothetical local equipment-maintenance policy* might define the canonical field `[WORK_ORDER][WO_017][STATUS]` with declared states `OPEN`, `TRIAGED`, and `CLOSED`. An authorized technician's documented classification might permit `OPEN → TRIAGED`; `OPEN → CLOSED` would be forbidden without the locally specified completion evidence and verification. An LLM may propose the transition, but it neither defines the institution's policy nor acquires commit authority by suggesting the next status. The domain expert must decide what evidence is sufficient, whose approval counts, whether exceptions are lawful, and how those rules are audited. These example states are illustrative, not a universal or field-certified maintenance standard.

Taken together, these examples establish *additional categories of work* for experts who choose to participate in AI governance. They do not establish that the profession-wide labor effect is additive. Practitioners may specialize in constitutional authorship, square maintenance, evaluation, local adaptation, or inter-domain negotiation, while other tasks become cheaper or disappear. How these activities translate into positions depends on reuse, deployment scale, regulatory obligations, and where human judgment remains necessary. Section 5 makes those dependencies explicit rather than treating required authority as a one-to-one employment multiplier.

At institutional scale, professional societies and educational institutions could extend familiar work — standards development, peer review, accreditation, dispute resolution, training — to govern square construction and maintenance. These are plausible organizational roles, not established future staffing outcomes. Whether an institution chooses centralized reusable standards, local variants, specialized review teams, or other arrangements is a domain-governance decision outside what the rectangle can prescribe.

## 5. The Reuse and Apprenticeship Questions

The **reuse objection** is real. One founder or committee may render a reusable square, and one qualification effort may support many deployments of the same configuration. OTTC does not require a new founder for every copy or a fresh human signature on every transaction. The countervailing work is domain-local adaptation, evidence and permission rules that vary by institution or jurisdiction, change control as law and practice evolve, testing new model versions, incident review, and negotiated cross-square boundaries. Some functions scale with the number of *distinct lawful contexts* or subsequent changes; others are reusable. The balance cannot be derived from architecture alone.

The **maturation stage** changes where authority is exercised. In Stages 1 and 2, when governance remains in-model or is split across model roles, each consequential deployment still needs accountable operational authority and oversight appropriate to its domain; written rules alone do not supply reliable runtime governance. At Stage 3, deterministic external stores and admission gates can enforce committed rules without a human manually approving each individual write. Human expertise remains indispensable to constitutional authorship, local configuration, assurance, maintenance, escalation, and professional judgment at the practice surface, but it need not be invoked as a per-event approval ritual. Reuse may reduce some labor and leave or create other labor. Neither indefinite manual gatekeeping nor mass employment follows by necessity.

The **apprenticeship objection** is different. The LLM at the proposer surface may take on document review, case workup, intermediate analysis, and first-draft reasoning — precisely the work through which novices have traditionally learned to judge. Residents become attending physicians partly by working up cases; junior attorneys develop judgment through supervised drafting and review; graduate students mature through experiments, analysis, and writing. If automation removes practice opportunities without replacing their educational function, the supply of future authorities becomes a serious concern.

This creates an apprenticeship risk and a possible mismatch between demand for mature authorities and the supply of new ones. A square needs lawful domain authority, and a federation needs independently authorized participants at negotiated boundaries, but a reusable deployment does not necessarily need a separate founder or full-time expert for each copy. At the same time, automation can reduce the supervised entry-level work that previously helped produce judgment. Institutional continuity does not by itself guarantee a substitute learning path. A skeptic is right to demand evidence that novices can still acquire genuine domain competence under these altered conditions.

One answer the framework can propose — not yet a proven substitute for established training — is **supervised rendering apprenticeship**. The [method demonstration](./02%20Producing%20a%20Square%20A%20Method%20Demonstration.md) describes LLM proposals under a qualified domain expert's commit authority, with disagreements treated as diagnostic. An apprentice can examine proposed schemas, lawful transitions, evidentiary thresholds, and authority assignments, explain why a rule should be accepted or rejected, and have those judgments challenged by a senior practitioner *before* they affect real operations. This makes the construction of explicit governance a teaching surface for part of the judgment that traditional practice often conveys implicitly. It cannot replace all the encounters with real people, institutions, and consequences through which professional judgment develops.

This explicit channel has an additional advantage disclosed by the [tacit-completion failure mode in Document 02](./02%20Producing%20a%20Square%20A%20Method%20Demonstration.md). The founder's practiced judgment can make an incomplete rendering seem complete to its author. An apprentice who lacks that accumulated context may ask, "Where is the rule that actually authorizes this change?" — a question that both teaches the apprentice and audits the founder's artifact. But this works only if the senior expert treats missing rules as design defects instead of supplying answers orally forever. The rendered constitution can transmit only what has been rendered. Supervised real-domain practice remains necessary to expose exceptions, tacit norms, and situations the written specification has yet to capture. Rendering apprenticeship is therefore a plausible complement and redesign of the traditional ladder, with a distinct value in making judgment explicit; calling it categorically *better* would require comparative evidence.

Three honest limits attach to this proposal. First, it is a structural training design, not a validated professional program; whether supervised rendering develops competence as reliably as traditional routes requires longitudinal assessment, including real-world judgment outside the documents. Second, tacit completion can survive even expert-approved text, so outside reviewers and apprentices must test whether consequential rules actually appear in the artifact. Third, the approach depends on experienced practitioners willing and able to supervise while their own tacit expertise remains available. That creates a time-sensitive opportunity to develop and evaluate such training before major changes to entry-level practice make supervision harder to arrange. It is not evidence that one uniform apprenticeship model will fit every domain.

The labor picture, corrected, is conditional but substantial. Governance of consequential state calls for real expert authority, yet reusable squares and Stage 3 automation prevent a simple one-founder-per-deployment or one-approver-per-commit employment forecast. Where work scales, it is through distinct domain contexts, evolving standards, assurance, boundary negotiation, and professional practice. Meanwhile professions must protect the cultivation of future authorities as entry-level proposer tasks change. Supervised rendering is a testable part of that answer, not an established replacement for all apprenticeship. The framework can legitimately offer new kinds of professional work without promising that net employment necessarily expands.

## 6. Multi-Disciplinary Work

Federation between squares requires inter-domain expert-pair work: two domain experts negotiating which referents between their fields are genuinely shared, which only appear shared, which require translation rules, which are non-translatable and must be marked as such. The federation paper develops what this work involves architecturally. This paper develops what it implies for labor.

Inter-domain negotiation is a distinct potential category of professional labor. It is not internal to either domain: experts with legitimate authority must decide which referents are shared, which translations are admissible, and which are prohibited or non-translatable. Such work resembles sustained collaborations between clinicians and bioethicists, attorneys and technical specialists, scientists and policy analysts, or educators and learning researchers. But negotiated rules may then be reused across many installations. Federation increases the number of *relationships between distinct authorities* that require initial and continuing governance; it does not imply that each data transfer requires a fresh expert pair or a new job. This work depends on the same mature authority whose apprenticeship pipeline Section 5 treats as an open question.

The institutional forms that might support this work are not for OTTC to dictate. Possible settings include committees between professional societies, treaty-like cross-domain agreements, research centers, or supervised training programs. Existing collaborations provide precedents, but some negotiated authority boundaries could require arrangements that do not yet exist. Which forms are warranted depends on adoption, domain law, professional politics, workload, and empirical performance. The framework can identify a *potential category* of inter-domain governance responsibilities and the authority they would require; it cannot establish in advance that those responsibilities will produce distinct careers or that existing institutions are necessarily inadequate.

One observation within the framework's scope is that genuine federation needs deep field expertise combined with sustained cross-disciplinary engagement. Some practitioners may specialize in within-field constitutional discovery; others in negotiating translation and admissibility between fields. These are coherent potential specialties, but whether they grow into durable career tracks depends on adoption, governance workload, and institutional choices. Both depend on a credible pathway for producing competent authorities rather than answering the apprenticeship question themselves.

Multi-disciplinary governance thus adds another *kind* of expert work, subject to the same reuse and automation caveats. Institutional arrangements might include negotiated standards, professional committees, joint review panels, and supervised training, but OTTC does not prescribe their form or promise a particular volume of positions. The framework can make boundary work legible and specify what lawful federation requires; professions must determine how those obligations are staffed and maintained.

## 7. Closing — An Invitation

The invitation this paper extends to practitioners in consequence-bearing fields rests on the authority they already hold, not on a promise of guaranteed employment expansion. Qualified experts are needed to define lawful domain structure, judge exceptions, supervise the development of successors, and decide how new governance responsibilities fit their professional institutions. Some will participate directly; others will exercise professional judgment by declining. Those choices remain theirs.

The invitation is not a demand. A practitioner may reasonably judge that a particular square is not worth building, that local conditions do not justify deployment, or that a claimed automation benefit fails under realistic scrutiny. The framework should welcome that judgment: without the authority to say no, expert participation is ceremonial. Where projects proceed, constitutional authorship and supervision are opportunities to extend professional practice, not mandates imposed by the technology.

The architecture makes new professional responsibilities visible: rendering lawful state transitions, maintaining domain-specific constitutions, qualifying systems, reviewing evidence and exceptions, negotiating between sovereign squares, and supervising novices. These may generate new careers or reorganize existing ones; their net labor effects cannot be established by constitutional theory alone. An empty rectangle cannot exercise a domain's judgment. A square exists only when authorized expertise supplies the ontology, boundaries, and lawful transitions; a federation exists only when authorities negotiate across those boundaries. The future form of that labor belongs to the professions and institutions that carry it.

The corpus offers a route for professional authority to remain constitutive as AI participation grows: not by denying task displacement, but by specifying where expert judgment must still define, authorize, maintain, and evaluate consequential state. It does not establish how many people institutions will employ, or whether a new apprenticeship will outperform an old one. Those questions require observation and accountable institutional choices. What the framework can do is make the governance work explicit enough that qualified practitioners can assess its value, test its boundaries, and decide whether to build it.

The rectangle does not decide who should practice a profession. It makes explicit the boundary at which that profession must decide what an AI system may commit.
