# Mutation Boundary Governance and the Future of Domain Expertise

## 1. The Displacement Frame

The dominant public frame for AI's relationship to professional labor is displacement. Every task an AI can do is a task a human used to do; every capability gain is a labor contraction. Practitioners reading this paper will have encountered some version of this frame, often repeatedly, and may bring its assumptions with them. Engaging seriously with their concerns is the right starting point. Dismissing the frame would be unserious, and would invite the dismissal the framework most needs to avoid: the assumption that AI advocates do not understand the labor stakes of what they are proposing.

The frame is not invented. It tracks a real concern, and it has produced a substantial body of analysis — automation forecasts, occupational impact studies, transition policy proposals — that takes the zero-sum dynamic seriously. Some of that analysis is well-reasoned, and some of it is alarmist; but neither extreme should obscure the reasonable underlying point. Some tasks within professional work will be reduced or eliminated by AI capability gains. Document review at scale. Certain forms of pattern recognition. Certain intermediate analytical products. Certain administrative workflows that currently consume professional time without requiring professional judgment. Practitioners who deny that any displacement will occur are not engaging seriously with the actual capability landscape, and the framework should not ask them to.

The frame is incomplete, however, in a specific way that this paper develops. It treats AI as a substitute for human capability — a system that does what humans previously did, faster or cheaper. For some tasks, this is the correct framing. But for consequence-bearing domains operating under mutation-boundary governance, the framing produces a different picture. AI participates in the domain as an opaque agent whose actions can affect durable shared state, and the work of governing that participation falls inevitably on the practitioners who already hold lawful authority in the field. AI in these domains does not substitute for domain expertise; it creates demand for domain expertise rendered into a new form. The practitioners are not displaced. They are required, in a role they alone can fulfill. What follows is not advocacy for any particular labor outcome. It is the labor picture that follows when the Mutation Boundary Problem is taken seriously and worked through to its consequences for who must do the governance work. If the framework's foundational claims hold, the labor picture this paper develops is what falls out of those claims rather than what the paper argues for.

The paper's claim is not that displacement does not happen. It is that for consequence-bearing domains operating under mutation-boundary governance, the labor picture is expansive rather than contractive. This is the picture practitioners should evaluate the framework by. The surface displacement picture, while it captures something real about other contexts, does not capture what happens when AI participates in domains whose governance requires the active commit authority of human experts. Different domains require different framings, and the framing this paper offers applies specifically to domains where consequence and governance are load-bearing.

## 2. Institutional Continuity

Every institution humans have built for governing consequential action across opaque agents — law, contract, peer review, parliamentary procedure, scientific publication, medical boards, professional licensure, accreditation, regulatory oversight — operates at the boundary between agents whose interiors are mutually inaccessible. A judge cannot inspect the interior reasoning of an advocate. A reviewer cannot inspect the interior process by which a researcher reached a conclusion. A medical board cannot inspect the diagnostic reasoning that produced a clinical decision. What these institutions can do is govern the boundary: require that arguments be presented in canonical form, audit transitions between provisional and committed claims, hold authorities accountable for their commitments, provide review mechanisms for retrospective correction. These are not imperfect substitutes for direct inspection of interiors. They are the correct site of governance between systems whose internal manifolds are opaque to each other, and they have been refined through centuries of institutional practice.

The Mutation Boundary Problem is the same problem applied to a new kind of agent. An LLM is opaque. Its interior is not accessible to outside observers in the way governance requires. Its outputs may become consequential in shared domains where lawful structure must be honored. The governance pattern that works for opaque human agents — render the boundary lawful, audit transitions, hold authority accountable — applies to opaque machine agents as well. The substrate is new. The pattern is ancient.

This means the corpus is not asking practitioners to learn AI. It is asking them to extend the institutional form their field already operates under to cover a new kind of participant in the field's consequential domains. A physician who participates in medical boards already understands what it means to govern an opaque agent — a colleague — whose interior reasoning cannot be directly inspected and whose actions can have durable consequences for patients. The board's mechanisms for credentialing, scope-of-practice review, malpractice adjudication, and disciplinary action are mutation-boundary governance applied to clinical decision-making. Extending that institutional form to govern AI participation in clinical care is continuous with what physicians already do.

The same continuity holds across professions. Attorneys operate within an institutional form — bar admission, professional responsibility rules, judicial review, appellate process — whose entire structure is mutation-boundary governance applied to legal reasoning. The court system does not inspect the interior of an advocate's mind; it governs the boundary at which legal claims become committed positions, requires those claims to be presented in canonical form, and provides review mechanisms when commitments later prove to have been wrongly made. Scientists operate within peer review and publication, which is mutation-boundary governance applied to scientific claims. The journal does not inspect the interior of a researcher's reasoning; it governs the boundary at which provisional findings become published claims that the field treats as part of the durable record. Researchers operate within IRB review and grant oversight, which is mutation-boundary governance applied to research conduct itself. Each profession's existing institutional form is a partial template for what AI participation in that profession requires.

What the corpus contributes is the pattern's articulation in a form that lets practitioners extend their existing institutional forms to AI participation rather than building new institutions from scratch. The constitutional-founder role this paper develops is therefore not a new role; it is an extension of the role practitioners already play within their professions, with one additional dimension: rendering their field's lawful structure into machine-legible form so that AI can participate under the field's authority rather than alongside it. Practitioners who serve on medical boards, who sit on peer review panels, who serve on bar committees, who participate in IRB review — these practitioners are already operating in the institutional form the framework asks them to extend. They are not being asked to become something other than what they are. They are being asked to extend their existing professional work into a new substrate.

## 3. The Constitutional-Founder Role

The corpus's commit-authority requirement places domain experts at the center of any legitimate square. Without a domain expert holding commit authority over their field's lawful structure, a square cannot exist. With one, the square can be produced through the disciplined collaboration the bridging document on producing a square develops in detail.

This role is what the paper calls constitutional founder. The domain expert is the authority who renders their field's lawful structure into machine-legible form, who governs that rendering's compliance with the rectangle's invariants, and who maintains commit authority over what the architecture actually does in their domain. The role is constitutional in the strict sense: the founder defines what counts as lawful within their domain, what authorities operate within it, what evidence governs change, what transitions are permitted. These are constitutional questions in any field, and AI participation does not change who has standing to answer them. They remain the practitioners' questions. The framework does not answer them; it provides the form in which the practitioners' answers can be rendered to govern AI participation.

The role is continuous with existing professional roles, with one new dimension. A physician who already holds clinical authority adds the dimension of rendering some aspect of that authority into a form that governs AI participation in clinical practice. An attorney who already holds professional authority adds the dimension of rendering some aspect of that authority into a form that governs AI participation in legal practice. A researcher who already holds research authority adds the dimension of rendering research-conduct standards into a form that governs AI participation in research. The expertise transfers. The discipline transfers. What is added is the rendering work and the maintenance work that follows from it.

The role does not require AI expertise. The bridging document on producing a square shows that LLMs serve as proposers under the practitioner's commit authority — supplying auxiliary technical content and architectural suggestions while the practitioner exercises their existing professional judgment to commit or reject. The practitioner does not need to learn machine learning, transformer architectures, or AI engineering to fulfill the role. They need to render their existing professional judgment into a form that LLMs can be governed by, and they need to maintain the discipline that keeps the rendering honest. Both skills are extensions of professional practice rather than departures from it. A physician rendering clinical decision-making into machine-legible form is doing essentially the same work they do when they articulate clinical reasoning for a resident, write a clinical guideline, or contribute to a board policy — with the additional constraint that the rendering must satisfy the rectangle's invariants and produce an architecture that governs AI participation lawfully.

The role is also institutional rather than individual at scale. A profession in which many practitioners hold constitutional-founder authority over their respective subdomains can negotiate inter-subdomain coordination through the same mechanisms it already uses — professional societies, standards committees, board structures, peer review processes. The framework does not require new institutions to handle the coordination work; it extends existing ones to cover AI participation in the institutional structures the profession already maintains. This is what makes the framework tractable for practitioners to engage with: they are not being asked to invent a new institutional form alongside their existing professional life. They are being asked to extend their existing professional life into a new dimension that operates within institutions they already participate in.

## 4. What Careers Look Like

A practicing physician under the framework continues to practice medicine. The framework does not replace clinical encounters, diagnostic judgment, treatment decisions, or therapeutic relationships. What it adds is the dimension of constitutional-founder work in their subdomain — rendering some aspect of their clinical lawful structure into a form that governs AI participation, and maintaining commit authority over the resulting architecture as it operates in practice. Some physicians will choose to do this work; many will not. Those who do will find that it composes with their existing practice rather than replacing parts of it. Clinical encounters continue. The constitutional-founder dimension operates alongside, in time the practitioner allocates between patient care and the rendering work the framework requires.

An attorney under the framework continues to practice law. The framework does not replace legal reasoning, advocacy, client relationships, or professional judgment. The constitutional-founder dimension, where it is engaged, takes the form of rendering some aspect of legal practice — perhaps procedure in a specific area of practice, perhaps standards for evidence handling, perhaps drafting protocols for specific document types — into machine-legible form, and governing the resulting architecture's operation under the attorney's commit authority. The practice of law continues. Some attorneys specialize in this work over time; others engage with it as one dimension among many.

A research scientist under the framework continues to do research. The constitutional-founder dimension, where engaged, takes the form of rendering some aspect of the field's research conduct — evidentiary standards, methodology requirements, replication protocols, peer-review procedures — into machine-legible form. Multi-disciplinary work, the agenda paper notes, is where Hypothesis Machines eventually become possible, but that is downstream. The near-term work is per-field constitutional discovery, done by practitioners in their own domains. Research continues. Constitutional-founder work supplements it for those who engage.

An educator under the framework continues to teach. This is worth dwelling on because educators face a particular version of the displacement frame — concerns about AI's role in classrooms, in assignment generation, in assessment, in tutoring relationships. The framework's offer to educators is the same offer it makes to practitioners in higher-stakes domains, with the same structure. An educator who chooses to engage with the constitutional-founder dimension renders some aspect of their pedagogical lawful structure into machine-legible form. What does it mean for a student to genuinely learn something in this discipline? What evidence warrants the judgment that learning has occurred? What pedagogical transitions are lawful — when can a student move on, when must they revisit, when has assessment been honest, when has it been performance? These are constitutional questions in education, and educators have always answered them through professional judgment. Rendering those answers into a form that governs AI participation in tutoring, assessment, or curriculum design is the constitutional-founder work for educators. The teaching itself continues. The work is additive, available to those educators who want to engage with it, optional for those who do not.

The educator case is particularly worth naming because educational stakes, while genuine, are different in character from clinical or legal stakes. An educator's decisions are usually correctable over longer time horizons than a clinician's. An educator's commit authority operates over learning trajectories rather than over patient outcomes. The proper-use boundary for educational squares looks different from the proper-use boundary for clinical squares — the kinds of mistakes that are tolerable, the kinds of governance that are required, the timing of human review. The constitutional-founder role adapts to these different stakes. The framework does not impose a single profile on every domain. Each domain's commit authority operates under conditions appropriate to that domain's actual consequence structure.

The labor picture is therefore additive rather than substitutive across all of these professional examples. Professionals continue to practice their professions. Some practitioners take up the constitutional-founder dimension as an additional facet of their work. Some specialize in it more heavily over time. New career paths emerge — practitioners who do constitutional-founder work primarily and clinical or legal or educational practice secondarily, practitioners who specialize in maintaining and updating squares as their fields evolve, practitioners who serve as inter-domain expert-pair members negotiating canonical-dictionary entries between adjacent fields. None of this displaces existing professional roles. All of it adds dimensions that practitioners can engage with according to their interests, capacities, and career trajectories.

The institutional dimension follows from the additive picture. Professional societies acquire a new form of work: convening practitioners around constitutional-founder questions, mediating disputes about canonical-dictionary entries, accrediting squares for use in their domains, maintaining the constitutional record as fields evolve. None of this is unprecedented; it extends the work professional societies already do. The work creates careers within those societies as well — administrative, scholarly, mediating, accrediting roles that grow as the federation matures. Educational institutions acquire a parallel new form of work: training practitioners in the constitutional-founder dimension, developing curricula for inter-domain pair work, accrediting programs that prepare practitioners for the role. Again, none of this is unprecedented; it extends the work educational institutions already do.

## 5. Multi-Disciplinary Work

Federation between squares requires inter-domain expert-pair work: two domain experts negotiating which referents between their fields are genuinely shared, which only appear shared, which require translation rules, which are non-translatable and must be marked as such. Paper 3 develops what this work involves architecturally. This paper develops what it implies for labor.

The pair work is its own category of professional labor. It is not internal to either domain; it is the work done at the boundary between domains. The practitioners who do it must be deeply credentialed in their own fields and able to engage seriously with peers from adjacent fields. This is a particular kind of expertise — domain-anchored but cross-disciplinary in practice — that few institutions currently train for explicitly. It is not the same as interdisciplinary training that produces shallow generalists; it is closer to the model of senior practitioners in adjacent fields who develop sustained collaborative relationships over careers. Physicians who work with bioethicists on research design. Attorneys who work with technical experts in patent litigation. Scientists who work with policy analysts on regulatory science. Educators who work with cognitive scientists on assessment design. Federation work extends and formalizes this model.

The institutional forms that will eventually support this work are not for this paper to prescribe. Whatever they end up looking like — joint committees between professional societies, formal treaty-style negotiations between fields, scholarly conferences dedicated to canonical-dictionary work, research centers organized around specific cross-domain pairs, training programs that prepare practitioners explicitly for the role — the framework has nothing to add to the question of how such institutions should be organized. That is human-governance work, contested, dependent on the political and professional realities of each pair of fields involved, and beyond the framework's standing to recommend. What the framework can name is that the work exists, that it creates a category of professional labor that is currently underrecognized, and that practitioners who engage with it will find themselves doing work that has few existing analogues in formalized institutional life.

One observation that does fall within the framework's scope: the pair work creates demand for practitioners who hold both deep field-specific expertise and the disposition for sustained cross-disciplinary engagement. This is not a separate career track from the constitutional-founder role developed in Section 3; it is an extension of that role, available to practitioners who find themselves drawn to the boundary work between fields rather than the within-field rendering work. Some practitioners will gravitate toward in-field constitutional discovery. Others will gravitate toward inter-domain negotiation. Both are necessary; both create careers; neither displaces the other.

The labor picture for multi-disciplinary work is therefore a further expansion of the picture developed in earlier sections. Professionals continue to practice their professions. Constitutional-founder work emerges as one additional dimension. Inter-domain pair work emerges as another. Each adds rather than substitutes. The institutional forms that will support these new dimensions are not yet in place at scale, and how they ultimately take shape will depend on the practitioners who organize them — through whatever professional, scholarly, and political mechanisms their fields use to develop new institutional forms over time. The framework's role is to make the work tractable. The institutional shape the work eventually takes is the practitioners' to determine.

## 6. Closing — An Invitation

The invitation this paper extends is to practitioners in consequence-bearing fields, by virtue of the expertise they already hold. The framework needs constitutional founders. Only practitioners with deep field expertise can fulfill that role. The work is theirs to take up if they choose.

The invitation is not a demand. The framework will not arrive at scale through coercion or institutional fiat. It will arrive through practitioners who recognize that the work is available to them, that they are qualified to do it, and that doing it expands rather than contracts their professional identity. Practitioners who decline the invitation are not failing the framework; they are exercising the same professional judgment that has always been their prerogative. The framework respects that judgment. It simply notes that the work exists and that the practitioners who already hold lawful authority in their fields are the ones who can do it.

The work creates careers, expands fields, opens new institutional forms — but all of this requires the practitioners. Without them, the framework is empty rectangle. With them, it becomes the substrate for governed AI participation in consequence-bearing domains. The framework has done what it can do: articulated the rectangle's invariants, developed the method by which squares come into being, sketched the federation pattern that lets squares interact, named the labor picture that the work implies. What remains is the practitioners. The squares come from the experts. The federation comes from the inter-domain pairs. The institutional forms come from the professional and scholarly mechanisms each field uses to develop new institutions over time.

This is the labor picture the corpus offers professionals: continuity with the work they already do, extended into a new dimension, with the choice about whether to take up the extension remaining theirs. It is not the displacement picture. It is not a demand for retraining. It is not a claim that AI will substitute for what they do. It is an invitation to do work that genuinely expands what their professional expertise can govern, in the same institutional pattern their fields have always operated under. Practitioners who recognize the invitation and choose to take it up will find that the work is real, that the careers it creates are continuous with their existing professional lives, and that the framework treats their authority over their domains as constitutive rather than auxiliary. That treatment is not a rhetorical concession. It is the framework's central commitment about who has standing to determine what counts as lawful in consequence-bearing domains, and the framework cannot operate without it.

The invitation stands. The work is available. The practitioners are the ones who decide.
