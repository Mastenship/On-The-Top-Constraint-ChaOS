# Layer Distinction: How Interpretability and Boundary Governance Compose

## 1. The Misreading

The corpus's equivalence-principle framing is structurally similar to a move Einstein made in physics: refuse to dissolve a constitutive indistinguishability, and build the geometric theory on it. Applied to opaque generative systems, the move runs as follows. From the exterior of any opaque generator, no observation of output alone can determine which interior produced it. Two substrates with very different internal configurations can yield identical boundary behavior. This is constitutive opacity, not an engineering gap awaiting better instruments. Therefore governance must live at the boundary, because the boundary is the only available site at which an external observer can act.

This framing is sometimes read as polemical against interpretability. The misread runs roughly: the corpus says interpretability cannot solve the governance problem, therefore the corpus is opposed to interpretability research. The misread is invited by the corpus's compact rhetorical form. The equivalence-principle framing makes a strong negative claim about interpretability's relationship to governance, and the strong claim can read as adversarial without further unpacking.

The misread is wrong. The corpus's actual claim is layer-distinct. Governance is one research layer. Interpretability is another. The layers address different problems, and the corpus's claims about the governance layer do not reduce or oppose interpretability's claims about the substrate-intelligibility layer. Both layers can mature simultaneously. Both produce work that the other layer benefits from. The eventual mature architecture for opaque generative systems will use both. None of this is in tension with the equivalence-principle framing; it is in cooperation with it.

This paper develops the layer distinction at length, traces the technical mechanisms by which interpretability and governance compose, and addresses specific points where readers from either community may perceive friction that the cooperative reading dissolves. The paper is not a defense of either layer against the other. It is a positioning document for readers in both communities, written so that interpretability researchers can locate the framework's claims correctly, and so that governance practitioners can locate interpretability's contributions correctly. The discipline throughout is to make the layer distinction clear and trust the reader to recognize the cooperation, rather than to argue at length that the framework is not adversarial.

## 2. The Layer Distinction

Interpretability addresses substrate intelligibility. The research questions are about what is happening inside the model when it produces an output: what circuits are active, what features are being represented, what causal structure produces the model's behavior, how attention heads coordinate to produce specific kinds of outputs, what intermediate computations underlie the model's surface behavior. The work is concerned with making the model's internal computational process legible to outside observers, and the methodologies developed for it — mechanistic interpretability, activation analysis, circuit identification, sparse autoencoder feature discovery — are addressed to questions of internal mechanism.

Boundary governance addresses constitutional form. The research questions are about the institutional structure within which model output participates in consequential domains: under what conditions semantic generation may legitimately become durable consequence in a shared domain, what authorities operate at the boundary between proposal and commitment, what audit trail must be preserved, what proper-use limits constrain the architecture, how lawful structure in a domain is rendered into machine-legible form, who holds commit authority and how they exercise it. The work is concerned with the institutional and architectural conditions under which opaque generative systems can participate in shared reality without dissolving it, and the methodologies developed for it — propose-commit separation, canonical addressing, perspectivism on relational state, federation across squares, qualification assays — are addressed to questions of institutional form rather than internal mechanism.

These are different research questions. Even with perfect interpretability — a complete causal account of every model output — the question of what should become durable state in a given domain, under whose authority, with what audit closure, with what proper-use boundary, would remain. Interpretability tells you what happened inside the model. Governance tells you what should be authorized as consequence. Knowing what happened does not answer what should be authorized. The latter is a constitutional question that requires domain expertise and lawful structure, not interior visibility. A physician who knows exactly how an AI's reasoning produced a treatment recommendation still has to decide whether the recommendation is appropriate to authorize as a clinical commitment. The interior knowledge informs the decision. It does not make the decision. The making of the decision is the constitutional work, and constitutional work happens at the boundary.

The medical-imaging-versus-surgical-protocol analogy that the agenda paper develops in Section 3 captures this layer distinction precisely. Medical imaging tells the surgeon what is inside the patient. Surgical protocol tells the surgeon what to do given what is inside. Better imaging makes better protocols possible. The protocols do not become unnecessary as imaging improves; they become better-targeted. The same composition holds between interpretability and governance. Better interpretability makes better governance possible. Governance does not become unnecessary as interpretability matures; it becomes better-informed.

The layer distinction also runs the other direction. Governance frames the questions interpretability answers. Without governance structure, interpretability is the study of arbitrary internal dynamics — interesting and important, but unfocused with respect to what its discoveries are for. With governance structure, interpretability has named features to track. Does this attention head correspond to canonical-address recognition? Does this circuit implement the propose-versus-commit distinction? Does this representation honor the visibility-class boundary between character cognition and governance state? When the architecture detects a forbidden-alias surfacing, what activation pattern produced it? The constitutional vocabulary becomes a target language for interpretability claims. Governance frames; interpretability answers; both are needed for the eventual mature picture, and each has work the other cannot do.

The cooperative reading is not a rhetorical concession. It is what falls out of the layer distinction once the distinction is made clear. Interpretability is not a path to governance because interpretability addresses a different layer. Governance is not a substitute for interpretability because governance addresses a different layer. The two compose, and the composition is what the eventual mature architecture for opaque generative systems will rest on.

## 3. Technical Composition Points

The layer distinction becomes operational when it is traced through specific mechanisms by which interpretability and governance compose. Five composition points are worth developing concretely, because each shows where the cooperative reading produces something neither layer would produce alone.

The first composition point is **time-axis collapse on tidal-force measurement.** The substrate physics paper develops the argument that long-horizon assays are needed to distinguish deep binding from surface compliance. A trajectory that genuinely tracks the architecture's engineered field looks behaviorally similar, in short evaluation windows, to a trajectory that happens to land in the prescribed region without being deflected there by the architecture. The distinction emerges over extended observation, under varied conditions, where the trajectory that was merely coincident eventually diverges from the trajectory that was actually bound. The assay regime is the protocol for performing this measurement; long-horizon, blind, varied-condition assays are the load-bearing instrument. Interpretability, where it succeeds in making activation-level binding directly observable, collapses the time-axis on this measurement. What the long-horizon assay regime distinguishes through behavioral inference, interpretability tools can in principle distinguish through direct inspection of internal representations. The activation pattern that corresponds to canonical-address binding is observable in a single pass; behavioral assays must run long enough for surface compliance to come apart from deep binding under pressure. This is not a replacement of one method by another. The behavioral assays remain valid; they are now augmented by interpretability evidence that addresses the same question through a different vantage point. The two methods compose to give the architect higher confidence with lower observation cost.

The second composition point is **Stage 3 promotion decisions.** The maturation doctrine identifies promotion candidates: governance moves that are stable enough across substrates and over time to be hardened from corpus-mass to deterministic external enforcement. The key safety question for any candidate is whether the slope is deeply binding or shallowly binding. Promoting a shallow-binding move freezes surface compliance into hard enforcement, and discovers when the corpus-mass scaffold is removed that the underlying trajectory was never bound at all. The cost of a wrong promotion decision is significant: an architecture that appeared to be working under corpus-mass governance reveals, after the scaffold is removed, that the binding was an artifact of the scaffold rather than a structural property of the substrate. Long-horizon assays can detect this through behavioral evidence accumulated over extended deployment. Interpretability, where available, can detect it through direct activation evidence. Both methods produce the kind of evidence Stage 3 promotion requires, and they compose: a candidate move that passes both behavioral and interpretability checks for deep binding is safer to promote than one that passes only one. The architect making promotion decisions benefits from convergent evidence across the two methods; where they agree, the promotion is robust; where they diverge, the divergence is itself diagnostic of something the architect should investigate before promoting.

The third composition point is **forbidden-alias diagnostics.** The substrate physics paper names the pattern of explicit prohibition placed in local context as anti-mass: naming the prose temptation and subtracting its mass at the competing well. Interpretability research that identifies which token sequences activate which circuits can inform which forbidden aliases need to be named and where they need to be placed. A model whose interior reveals strong activation of a particular prose temptation in proposal-generation contexts is a model whose governance architecture should explicitly forbid that alias before it surfaces. The behavioral assay regime can detect such surfacings post-hoc, when they appear in output and trigger violation flags. Interpretability can detect the temptation upstream, before it surfaces, by observing the activation patterns associated with the prose temptation in the proposal-generation context. The two methods are diagnostic at different points in the trajectory: interpretability catches the prior pulling toward a forbidden well before the trajectory reaches the boundary; assays catch the trajectory crossing the boundary in violation. Architecture that uses both has earlier warning of where the substrate's continuation distribution is exerting force the governance has not yet adequately countered.

The fourth composition point is **substrate qualification.** The framework's claim that coupling varies by substrate, by language, and by token form is testable through both behavioral assays and interpretability methods. Behavioral assays measure deflection on the qualification battery — CAA scores, write-surface obedience under stress, epistemic-boundary integrity, audit non-theater. Interpretability methods measure attention-head behavior under canonical token sequences, activation patterns associated with governance-relevant features, representation of canonical addresses and forbidden aliases at the circuit level. The two methods produce convergent evidence about substrate fit. Where they agree, the qualification is robust and the architect has high confidence the substrate is suitable for the architecture. Where they diverge, the divergence is itself diagnostic. A substrate that passes behavioral assays but reveals weak interior representation of governance features under interpretability inspection is a substrate where the surface compliance may not survive at scale or under adversarial pressure. A substrate that fails behavioral assays but reveals strong interior representation of governance features under interpretability inspection is a substrate where the rendering may need adjustment to better couple to features the substrate already has. The divergence cases are where the layer composition is most informative; agreement cases are reassuring but reveal less about where the architecture-substrate interaction needs work.

The fifth composition point is **visibility-class verification.** The architecture's read-visibility-class distinction operates as causal stratification: certain regions of the manifold must remain causally inaccessible to certain other regions. In ChaOS, this distinction separates CHARACTER_VISIBLE state from GOVERNANCE_INTERNAL state, and a violation of the stratification — a character-cognition COT pulling state from the governance layer into impulse generation — is a category leak that would corrupt both layers. Behaviorally, this is verified post-hoc through CASCADE_TRACE inspection: did any COT in the character cognition band reference governance-internal state in its reasoning? Interpretability, where available, can verify the stratification directly by inspecting whether the relevant attention heads do or do not attend across the stratification boundary. The behavioral and interpretability methods compose to give the architect higher confidence that the visibility class is being honored at the substrate level rather than merely at the output surface. A character-cognition output that appears clean in CASCADE_TRACE but reveals attention to governance-internal features under interpretability inspection is an output where the surface stratification may not be load-bearing under pressure; the leak is happening at the attention level even if it has not yet reached the output. Together, the two methods catch what neither catches alone.

These five composition points share a structural pattern. In each case, behavioral methods and interpretability methods produce evidence about the same underlying question — whether the architecture's intended structure is operative at the substrate level. The methods access the question from different vantage points: behavioral methods observe output trajectories under varied conditions, interpretability methods observe internal computational structure under specific inputs. Both vantage points are valid. Their composition is what makes the eventual mature evaluation regime stronger than either alone, and the composition does not require one method to subordinate to the other. They are layered methods addressing layered aspects of the same architecture-substrate interaction.

## 4. Where the Layer Distinction Matters Most

Four specific points are where the cooperative reading is most at risk of breaking down for readers who have not internalized the layer distinction. Each is worth addressing explicitly, technically, and without rhetorical defense.

The strongest perceived friction is the equivalence principle's claim that no observation of output alone can determine which interior produced it. This sounds adversarial to interpretability because interpretability's project is precisely to inspect interiors. But the claim is not about interpretability's project. It is about the external observer who must govern the boundary. From outside the model — which is the governance layer's vantage point, where the operative observers are domain experts, audit systems, and downstream consumers of the model's commitments — output alone is insufficient to determine interior structure, and this insufficiency is what makes governance at the boundary necessary rather than optional. From inside the model, which is interpretability's vantage point, output is not the only available signal. Activations, attention patterns, and circuit-level structure are also accessible, and interpretability's research program is precisely the development of methods for accessing them. The equivalence principle does not deny this. It says that for the governance layer's purposes, where the operative observer is necessarily external to the model in deployment, the indistinguishability holds and governance must be designed under that constraint. Interpretability operates at a different vantage point and is not constrained by the same indistinguishability. The two claims are about different observers with different access conditions, and neither contradicts the other.

A related point is that the corpus's claim that interpretability is not the path to governance can read as dismissing interpretability's relevance to governance entirely. The actual claim is narrower. Interpretability cannot substitute for governance because the governance layer's questions — what should be authorized as consequence, under whose authority, with what audit closure, with what proper-use boundary — are not answered by interior visibility alone. A complete causal account of the model's reasoning does not by itself determine what should become durable state in a given domain. The latter is a constitutional question requiring domain expertise and lawful structure, and the former does not produce it as output. Interpretability can inform governance by clarifying what the model is actually doing when it produces a proposal. It can accelerate governance by collapsing time-axes on assay measurements as Section 3 develops. It can validate governance choices by providing convergent evidence that intended bindings are operative at the substrate level. None of these are substitution; all of them are cooperation. The not-the-path framing is about substitution rather than about cooperation, and reading it as opposition misses the operative distinction.

A third point is the asymmetry of maturation timelines, which is sometimes a source of perceived friction. Boundary governance is buildable now, with the architectures the corpus describes, by domain experts working with corpus-loaded LLMs. The bridging document on producing a square develops the method in detail. Frontier-scale interpretability is harder and slower; the techniques that work at small scale and on toy problems do not yet generalize to frontier models with full reliability, and the research program required to close that gap is substantial. A reader from interpretability research may perceive the corpus as claiming priority for governance work because governance is currently more available. The cooperative reading does not require this priority claim. Both research programs proceed at their own paces. The composition between them becomes more powerful as both mature, and neither needs to be complete for the other to proceed productively. Governance work that proceeds without yet-mature interpretability is not making a claim that interpretability is unnecessary or secondary; it is doing the work that is currently buildable, in the layer where it is currently buildable. As interpretability matures, the composition described in Section 3 becomes operative, and the architecture benefits from both layers maturing in parallel rather than sequentially. The corpus's current emphasis on governance reflects what the corpus is — a governance framework — rather than a claim about which research program matters more.

A fourth point: the corpus's silence on standard AI safety vocabulary is sometimes perceived as orthogonal to AI safety as a research field rather than complementary to it. The framework does not engage with deceptive alignment, mesa-optimization, capability overhang, sharp left turn, or the other framings that organize much of current AI safety research. This silence is sometimes read as the framework dismissing those concerns. The actual position is layer-distinct in a way analogous to the interpretability distinction. The framework addresses governance of opaque generative systems participating in consequential domains. The standard AI safety vocabulary addresses risks specific to advanced agentic systems pursuing potentially misaligned objectives. These are different research programs operating under different framings, and the framework's silence on the AI safety vocabulary is not opposition but the recognition that a different research program addresses a different question. Both can be valid; neither requires the other to engage with it on its terms. A practitioner working in AI safety who reads the corpus and finds none of their vocabulary should not infer that the corpus dismisses their concerns. They should infer that the corpus is doing different work, on a different layer, with cooperation possible where the layers compose. This section may benefit from revision in the future as the corpus is read more widely by AI safety researchers and the specific friction points become better understood through contact with that community's actual responses to the framework.

## 5. Closing — A Posture, Not a Position

The argument throughout this paper has been layer distinction. Interpretability addresses one layer of the opaque generative system problem. Boundary governance addresses another. The layers compose. Both research programs benefit from the composition. Neither is replaced or subsumed by the other.

The cooperative reading is not a position the paper advocates against alternatives. It is the disposition that follows from understanding what each research program is actually doing. Once the layer distinction is clear, cooperation is the natural posture, and what previously read as adversarial framing reads as straightforward technical claim about what governance work requires within its layer. The framework's strong claims are about its layer; its silences are about scope; its commitments are about the specific questions it addresses. Read this way, what initially appeared as opposition resolves into the kind of layered-research relationship that mature scientific and engineering programs typically have with adjacent programs operating at different layers of the same problem space.

The paper does not attempt to settle questions internal to interpretability research, internal to AI safety research, or internal to alignment research. It does not need to. Those research programs have their own internal questions, their own methodologies, their own communities, and the framework's relationship to each is layered rather than confrontational. What the paper offers is positioning. Interpretability researchers reading this can locate the framework's claims correctly: not as a competitor or alternative, but as a research program at a different layer that interpretability composes with cleanly when both are mature. AI safety researchers reading this can locate the framework's value-neutrality correctly: not as orthogonality with an implicit dismissal, but as a different kind of work addressed to a different question, where cooperation is possible at the points the layers touch. Governance practitioners reading this can locate interpretability's contributions correctly: not as a threat to governance's relevance, but as a research program whose maturation accelerates and validates governance choices through convergent evidence at the substrate level.

The closing posture is layer distinction making cooperation natural. The corpus's strong claims about governance are about governance, not against interpretability. The corpus's silence on AI safety vocabulary is about scope, not opposition. The corpus's commitment to boundary governance is about the specific layer the corpus addresses, not a claim that other layers do not matter. Read this way, the corpus and the broader AI safety and interpretability research communities are doing complementary work, and the eventual mature architecture for opaque generative systems will draw from all of them. The frameworks that succeed in the long arc of work on opaque generative systems will not be the ones that won against rival programs; they will be the ones that composed cleanly with adjacent work at adjacent layers. Mutation-boundary governance is designed for that composition. Interpretability and AI safety research are partners in it, not rivals against it.
