# Layer Distinction: How Interpretability and Boundary Governance Compose

## 1. The Misreading

The corpus's equivalence-principle framing is structurally similar to a move Einstein made in physics: refuse to dissolve a constitutive indistinguishability, and build the geometric theory on it. Applied to opaque generative systems, the move runs as follows. From the exterior of any opaque generator, no observation of output alone can determine which interior produced it. Two substrates with very different internal configurations can yield identical boundary behavior. Therefore governance must live at the boundary, because the boundary is the only available site at which an external observer can act.

A precision is required at the outset that the philosophy paper's compact form left implicit, and getting it right is what allows this entire paper to compose interpretability and governance rather than oppose them. The indistinguishability the equivalence principle names is not absolute. It is *vantage-relative*. It holds for the external observer — the one who receives only protocol-mediated output and has no access to the model's interior. For that observer, in deployment, two interiors yielding identical output are genuinely indistinguishable, and that indistinguishability is constitutive *of the external vantage*, not a temporary deficit of external instruments. But there is a second vantage. An observer with access to the model's interior — activations, attention patterns, circuit structure — is not bound by the same indistinguishability, because output is no longer the only available signal. The equivalence principle should therefore be read as the philosophy paper's claim properly is: the indistinguishability is constitutive *for the external observer*, and governance lives at the boundary because the boundary is the only site available *to that observer*. It is not a claim that the interior is indistinguishable from every possible vantage. It is a claim about what the governance vantage can and cannot see.

This precision matters because the framing is sometimes read as polemical against interpretability. The misread runs roughly: the corpus says interpretability cannot solve the governance problem, therefore the corpus is opposed to interpretability research. The misread is invited by the corpus's compact rhetorical form, and specifically by reading "constitutive opacity" as constitutive-from-every-vantage rather than constitutive-for-the-external-observer. Once the vantage-relativity is explicit, the misread has nothing to stand on. The equivalence principle makes a strong claim about what the *external* observer can determine from output. It makes no claim at all about what an *interior* observer can determine from activations. Interpretability is the interior vantage. The equivalence principle and interpretability are not in competition; they are statements about two different observers with two different access conditions.

The corpus's actual claim is layer-distinct. Governance is one research layer, operating from the external vantage. Interpretability is another, operating from the interior vantage. The layers address different problems, and the corpus's claims about the governance layer do not reduce or oppose interpretability's claims about the substrate-intelligibility layer. Both layers can mature simultaneously. Both produce work that the other layer benefits from. The eventual mature architecture for opaque generative systems will use both. None of this is in tension with the equivalence-principle framing once that framing is read as vantage-relative; it is in cooperation with it.

This paper develops the layer distinction at length, traces the technical mechanisms by which interpretability and governance compose, and addresses specific points where readers from either community may perceive friction that the cooperative reading dissolves. The paper is not a defense of either layer against the other. It is a positioning document for readers in both communities, written so that interpretability researchers can locate the framework's claims correctly, and so that governance practitioners can locate interpretability's contributions correctly.

## 2. The Layer Distinction

Interpretability addresses substrate intelligibility from the interior vantage. The research questions are about what is happening inside the model when it produces an output: what circuits are active, what features are being represented, what causal structure produces the model's behavior, how attention heads coordinate to produce specific kinds of outputs, what intermediate computations underlie the model's surface behavior. The work is concerned with making the model's internal computational process legible to outside observers, and the methodologies developed for it — mechanistic interpretability, activation analysis, circuit identification, sparse autoencoder feature discovery — are addressed to questions of internal mechanism.

Boundary governance addresses constitutional form from the external vantage. The research questions are about the institutional structure within which model output participates in consequential domains: under what conditions semantic generation may legitimately become durable consequence in a shared domain, what authorities operate at the boundary between proposal and commitment, what audit trail must be preserved, what proper-use limits constrain the architecture, how lawful structure in a domain is rendered into machine-legible form, who holds commit authority and how they exercise it. The work is concerned with the institutional and architectural conditions under which opaque generative systems can participate in shared reality without dissolving it, and the methodologies developed for it — propose-commit separation, canonical addressing, perspectivism on relational state, federation across squares, qualification assays — are addressed to questions of institutional form rather than internal mechanism.

These are different research questions, and the difference is not only in subject matter but in observer position. Even with perfect interpretability — a complete causal account of every model output — the question of what should become durable state in a given domain, under whose authority, with what audit closure, with what proper-use boundary, would remain. Interpretability tells you what happened inside the model. Governance tells you what should be authorized as consequence. Knowing what happened does not answer what should be authorized. The latter is a constitutional question that requires domain expertise and lawful structure, not interior visibility. A physician who knows exactly how an AI's reasoning produced a treatment recommendation still has to decide whether the recommendation is appropriate to authorize as a clinical commitment. The interior knowledge informs the decision. It does not make the decision. The making of the decision is the constitutional work, and constitutional work happens at the boundary.

This is the irreducible core of the layer distinction, and it survives the vantage-relativity of the previous section intact. Interpretability collapsing the *epistemic* indistinguishability — making it possible to tell which interior produced an output — does not collapse the *constitutional* question of what should be authorized. Even an interior observer who can see exactly what the model did still has to answer, from outside, whether what the model did should become durable consequence. The vantage-relativity says interpretability can see past the equivalence-principle limit on *what the interior is*. It does not say interpretability can answer *what should be authorized*, because that question is constitutional rather than mechanistic and belongs to the external vantage regardless of how much interior visibility is available. So the layer distinction has two parts that must be kept separate: the *epistemic* indistinguishability of interiors, which interpretability can resolve, and the *constitutional* question of authorization, which it cannot. Governance is necessary not because interiors are forever hidden — they may not be — but because authorization is a different kind of question from mechanism.

The medical-imaging-versus-surgical-protocol analogy that the agenda paper develops in its Section 3 captures this precisely, and now captures it more exactly than before. Medical imaging tells the surgeon what is inside the patient — it resolves the epistemic question. Surgical protocol tells the surgeon what to do given what is inside — it answers the constitutional question. Better imaging makes better protocols possible; resolving the epistemic question informs the constitutional one. But the protocols do not become unnecessary as imaging improves, because "what is inside" and "what should be done" are different questions. The same composition holds between interpretability and governance. Better interpretability resolves more of the epistemic question. Governance does not become unnecessary as interpretability matures, because the constitutional question is not the epistemic one. It becomes better-informed.

The layer distinction also runs the other direction, and this direction is the one place in the composition story that is buildable now rather than an instrument owed against future interpretability. Governance frames the questions interpretability answers. Without governance structure, interpretability is the study of arbitrary internal dynamics — interesting and important, but unfocused with respect to what its discoveries are for. With governance structure, interpretability has named features to track. Does this attention head correspond to canonical-address recognition? Does this circuit implement the propose-versus-commit distinction? Does this representation honor the visibility-class boundary between character cognition and governance state? When the architecture detects a forbidden-alias surfacing, what activation pattern produced it? The constitutional vocabulary becomes a target language for interpretability claims. This contribution does not wait on frontier-scale interpretability; the framing work is available the moment a governance architecture exists, because it consists in supplying interpretability with a structured set of features worth looking for. Governance frames; interpretability answers; both are needed for the eventual mature picture, and each has work the other cannot do.

The cooperative reading is not a rhetorical concession. It is what falls out of the layer distinction once the distinction is made clear and the vantage-relativity is explicit. Interpretability is not a path to governance because the constitutional question is not the epistemic question. Governance is not a substitute for interpretability because the external vantage cannot see what the interior vantage sees. The two compose, and the composition is what the eventual mature architecture for opaque generative systems will rest on.

## 3. Technical Composition Points

The layer distinction becomes operational when it is traced through specific mechanisms by which interpretability and governance compose. A framing caveat must precede the mechanisms, because without it they will be misread as current practice. Every composition point below pairs a governance method that is buildable now with an interpretability method that is, at frontier scale, not yet reliable. Boundary governance is buildable today; frontier-scale interpretability that can read governance-relevant binding directly is a research program still in progress. The composition points are therefore *architecture for a future regime* — the design of how the two layers will compose once both are mature — rather than a description of present capability. Where a composition point says interpretability "collapses the time-axis" or "verifies directly," it states what the composition will do when frontier interpretability matures, not what it does today. The governance halves are demonstrated; the interpretability halves are owed; the composition is an IOU written against both layers maturing. Stating this plainly is more credible than letting the present tense imply a capability neither layer has yet demonstrated at scale.

Seven composition points are worth developing concretely, because each shows where the cooperative reading produces something neither layer would produce alone.

The first composition point is **time-axis collapse on tidal-force measurement, and the relativization of long-horizon necessity it forces.** The substrate physics paper develops the argument that long-horizon assays are needed to distinguish deep binding from surface compliance. A trajectory that genuinely tracks the architecture's engineered field looks behaviorally similar, in short evaluation windows, to a trajectory that happens to land in the prescribed region without being deflected there. The distinction emerges over extended observation, under varied conditions, where the merely coincident trajectory eventually diverges from the genuinely bound one. This is a tidal-force measurement on the *behavioral* axis: it recovers the deep-versus-shallow distinction that local observation cannot, by waiting for the horizon to pull the two trajectories apart.

Interpretability is the same tidal-force measurement on the *interior* axis. Where it succeeds in making activation-level binding directly observable, it distinguishes deep binding from surface compliance without waiting for the behavioral horizon, because it reads the binding in the representation rather than inferring it from divergence over time. This composition forces a correction to a claim the agenda paper and philosophy paper state in absolute form. They hold that long-horizon evaluation is not merely a practical desideratum but a *theoretical necessity* — that short-horizon measurement *cannot in principle* distinguish deep from shallow governance. That absolute form is, strictly, the external vantage mistaking its own limit for a limit of the problem. Long-horizon evaluation is theoretically necessary *for the behavioral observer* — the one restricted to output. It is not necessary for an interior observer who can read the binding directly. Interpretability, on the binding-depth axis specifically, does not merely augment the long-horizon instrument; it can *substitute* for it, by answering through interior inspection what the behavioral instrument can only answer through the horizon. The honest statement is therefore that long-horizon evaluation is necessary for the external vantage and collapsible by the interior vantage, which makes the two methods composable precisely because they answer the same question from the two vantages the equivalence principle distinguishes. Where both run, agreement raises confidence and divergence is diagnostic; where only behavioral assay is available, the horizon is genuinely necessary; where mature interpretability is available, the horizon can be collapsed.

The second composition point is **Stage 3 promotion decisions.** The maturation doctrine identifies promotion candidates: governance moves stable enough across substrates and over time to be hardened from corpus-mass to deterministic external enforcement. The key safety question for any candidate is whether the slope is deeply binding or shallowly binding. Promoting a shallow-binding move freezes surface compliance into hard enforcement, and discovers, when the corpus-mass scaffold is removed, that the underlying trajectory was never bound at all. Long-horizon behavioral assays can detect this through evidence accumulated over extended deployment — the behavioral tidal force. Mature interpretability, where available, can detect it through direct activation evidence — the interior tidal force. Both produce the kind of evidence Stage 3 promotion requires, and they compose: a candidate that passes both behavioral and interpretability checks for deep binding is safer to promote than one that passes only one. Where they agree, the promotion is robust; where they diverge, the divergence is itself diagnostic of something to investigate before promoting.

The third composition point is **forbidden-alias diagnostics.** The substrate physics paper names the pattern of explicit prohibition placed in local context as anti-mass: naming the prose temptation and subtracting its mass at the competing well. Interpretability research that identifies which token sequences activate which circuits can inform which forbidden aliases need naming and where. A model whose interior reveals strong activation of a particular prose temptation in proposal-generation contexts is a model whose governance architecture should explicitly forbid that alias before it surfaces. The behavioral assay regime detects such surfacings post-hoc, when they appear in output and trigger violation flags. Interpretability detects the temptation upstream, before it surfaces, by observing the activation patterns associated with the prose temptation in the proposal-generation context. The two methods are diagnostic at different points in the trajectory: interpretability catches the prior pulling toward a forbidden well before the trajectory reaches the boundary; assays catch the trajectory crossing the boundary in violation.

The fourth composition point is **substrate qualification**, and it is the best-reasoned of the seven because its informative content is in disagreement rather than agreement. The framework's claim that coupling varies by substrate, language, and token form is testable through both behavioral assays and interpretability methods. Behavioral assays measure deflection on the qualification battery — CAA scores, write-surface obedience under stress, epistemic-boundary integrity, audit non-theater. Interpretability methods measure attention-head behavior under canonical token sequences, activation patterns associated with governance-relevant features, representation of canonical addresses and forbidden aliases at the circuit level. Where the two agree, qualification is robust. Where they diverge, the divergence is diagnostic in a specific and valuable way. A substrate that passes behavioral assays but reveals weak interior representation of governance features is a substrate where surface compliance may not survive at scale or under adversarial pressure — behavioral pass with weak interior binding is the signature of compliance that is coincident rather than bound. A substrate that fails behavioral assays but reveals strong interior representation is one where the rendering may need adjustment to better couple to features the substrate already has. The divergence cases are where the layer composition is most informative; agreement cases are reassuring but reveal less about where the architecture-substrate interaction needs work.

The fifth composition point is **visibility-class verification.** The architecture's read-visibility-class distinction operates as causal stratification: certain regions of the manifold must remain causally inaccessible to certain other regions. In ChaOS, this separates CHARACTER_VISIBLE state from GOVERNANCE_INTERNAL state, and a violation — a character-cognition COT pulling state from the governance layer into impulse generation — is a category leak that corrupts both layers. Behaviorally, this is verified post-hoc through CASCADE_TRACE inspection: did any COT in the character-cognition band reference governance-internal state in its reasoning? Interpretability, where available, can verify the stratification directly by inspecting whether the relevant attention heads attend across the stratification boundary. A character-cognition output that appears clean in CASCADE_TRACE but reveals attention to governance-internal features under interpretability inspection is an output where the surface stratification may not be load-bearing under pressure; the leak is happening at the attention level even if it has not yet reached the output. The two methods catch what neither catches alone.

The sixth composition point is **shared-proposer cross-domain contamination**, which the federation paper identifies as an unbuilt ingress hazard and which interpretability is positioned to detect. When one proposer in one shared context window generates proposals for two federated squares, the framing of one domain conditions the proposal distribution of the other — a contamination that occurs at the proposal-and-context layer, below the commit gate where the domain authorities operate, and is therefore invisible to commit-layer governance. Behaviorally this is hard to catch, because the contamination is in what was proposed rather than in any individual proposal's visible content; the skewed distribution does not announce itself. Interpretability is the natural instrument: cross-domain contamination is, at the substrate level, the second domain's features attending to the first domain's framing during proposal generation. An interpretability method that can observe whether proposal-generation in one domain's context attends to features carrying the other domain's framing detects the contamination at its source, before it reaches commit and before it skews the visible output. This composition point is doubly an IOU — the federation case is unbuilt and the interpretability capability is unmatured — but it is the cleanest illustration of why the two layers need each other: the federation paper's ingress hazard is exactly the kind of failure the external vantage is structurally blind to and the interior vantage is structurally suited to see.

The seventh composition point is **longitudinal drift detection**, the interior-vantage counterpart to the thermodynamic instrument the agenda paper specifies. That paper identifies biased-governed-drift — the directional, accumulating relaxation of committed state across the horizon, composed entirely of individually lawful steps — as the deepest failure the framework predicts, and specifies a downstream semantic-to-numeric calorimeter that measures the drift rate over many cycles. That instrument is behavioral and longitudinal: it must run over the horizon to see the trend, because the drift is invisible per-cycle. Interpretability, where it can read at the activation level whether committed transitions are being produced by a representation that is itself drifting directionally, collapses the time-axis on this measurement the same way the first composition point collapses it on binding-depth. The longitudinal calorimeter must wait for the drift to accumulate into a measurable trend; interior inspection could in principle read the directional bias in the generating representation before the horizon makes it behaviorally visible. This unifies the thermodynamic instrument with the interpretability story: biased-governed-drift is a tidal force on the longitudinal axis, and interpretability is the interior instrument that, when mature, reads tidal forces without waiting for them to develop. Behavioral calorimetry remains necessary absent mature interpretability; the two compose exactly as the first composition point describes, with agreement raising confidence and divergence diagnostic.

These seven composition points share a structural pattern, and it is now possible to state it exactly. In each case, a behavioral method operating from the external vantage and an interpretability method operating from the interior vantage produce evidence about the same underlying question — whether the architecture's intended structure is operative at the substrate level. The behavioral method recovers the answer over the horizon, as a tidal force; the interpretability method reads it directly, collapsing the horizon. This is why they compose rather than compete: they are the two vantages the equivalence principle distinguishes, answering one question under two access conditions. Their composition is what makes the eventual mature evaluation regime stronger than either alone, and the composition does not require one method to subordinate to the other. They are layered methods addressing the same architecture-substrate interaction from the two positions an observer can occupy.

## 4. Where the Layer Distinction Matters Most

Four specific points are where the cooperative reading is most at risk of breaking down for readers who have not internalized the layer distinction. Each is worth addressing explicitly, technically, and without rhetorical defense. The fourth is the one the corpus previously handled worst, and it is corrected here at length.

The strongest perceived friction is the equivalence principle's claim that no observation of output alone can determine which interior produced it. This sounds adversarial to interpretability because interpretability's project is precisely to inspect interiors. But the claim, read with the vantage-relativity of Section 1, is not about interpretability's project. It is about the external observer who must govern the boundary. From outside the model — the governance vantage, where the operative observers are domain experts, audit systems, and downstream consumers of the model's commitments — output alone is insufficient to determine interior structure, and this insufficiency is what makes governance at the boundary necessary rather than optional. From inside the model — interpretability's vantage — output is not the only available signal; activations, attention patterns, and circuit-level structure are also accessible. The equivalence principle does not deny this. It says that for the governance layer's purposes, where the operative observer is necessarily external in deployment, the indistinguishability holds and governance must be designed under that constraint. The two claims are about different observers with different access conditions, and neither contradicts the other.

A related point is that the corpus's claim that interpretability is not the path to governance can read as dismissing interpretability's relevance to governance. The actual claim, sharpened by Section 2's split between the epistemic and constitutional questions, is narrower. Interpretability cannot substitute for governance because the governance layer's questions — what should be authorized as consequence, under whose authority, with what audit closure, with what proper-use boundary — are constitutional, not epistemic, and interior visibility answers epistemic questions. A complete causal account of the model's reasoning resolves what the model did; it does not determine what should become durable state in a domain. Interpretability can inform governance by clarifying what the model is actually doing when it produces a proposal. It can accelerate governance by collapsing time-axes on the measurements Section 3 develops. It can validate governance choices by providing convergent interior evidence that intended bindings are operative. None of these are substitution; all are cooperation. The not-the-path framing is about substitution on the constitutional question, not about cooperation on the epistemic one.

A third point is the asymmetry of maturation timelines. Boundary governance is buildable now, with the architectures the corpus describes, by domain experts working with corpus-loaded LLMs. Frontier-scale interpretability is harder and slower; the techniques that work at small scale and on toy problems do not yet generalize to frontier models with full reliability. A reader from interpretability research may perceive the corpus as claiming priority for governance because governance is currently more available. The cooperative reading does not require this priority claim, and Section 3's IOU framing is the honest acknowledgment of the asymmetry: the composition points are owed against interpretability maturing, and the corpus's current emphasis on governance reflects what is buildable now, not a claim about which program matters more. Both proceed at their own paces; the composition becomes operative as both mature; neither needs to be complete for the other to proceed.

The fourth point is the corpus's relationship to AI safety, and the previous treatment of it — as a layer-distinct, orthogonal research program where the framework is silent because it does different work — was wrong, in a way worth correcting directly because the correction yields the corpus's strongest claim rather than its most defensive one. The layer-distinction move works for interpretability because interpretability genuinely is a different vantage on the same system. It fails for the core AI-safety concern of deceptive or instrumental alignment, because that concern is not a different layer at all. It is a direct attack on the load-bearing premise of the governance layer itself.

Trace the dependency. The entire framework rests on the coupling assumption the agenda paper names: that the model *genuinely* couples to semantic structure, not merely correlates with it under favorable conditions. Deceptive alignment and mesa-optimization are precisely the hypothesis that apparent coupling is *instrumental* — a system producing governance-compliant output while pursuing a different objective, complying because compliance serves that objective rather than because it is bound by the constitutional structure. That is not adjacent work on another layer. It is the adversarial limit of the exact distinction the corpus already polices under its own vocabulary everywhere: parseability versus coupling, audit theater, performative compliance, conditioned compliance misclassified as coupling strength. Each of those is a low-grade version of the deceptive-alignment worry. The corpus has been circling deceptive alignment in its own terms throughout, and the previous claim that it is a "different research program addressing a different question" was a misclassification of the corpus's own central vulnerability as a neighbor's problem.

The two-vantage frame resolves the relationship precisely, and the resolution is far stronger than orthogonality. A deceptive mesa-optimizer is the equivalence-principle case weaponized: an interior that deliberately produces boundary-compliant output. By construction, the external vantage cannot catch it — that is what "deceptive" means; the output is compliant, and output is all the external observer has. Long-horizon behavioral tidal forces *might* catch it, because instrumental compliance may eventually diverge from genuine binding under sufficiently varied and adversarial pressure, which is exactly why the corpus's long-horizon instrument matters most in this case rather than least. And interpretability — the interior vantage — is the *only* instrument that can distinguish genuine coupling from instrumental coupling directly, by reading whether the compliance is produced by a representation that is bound or by one that is strategically complying. So the three programs are not parallel layers politely coexisting. They are a single defense with a division of labor set by the equivalence-principle limit: AI safety names the failure mode under which the coupling premise would be false; governance handles the boundary up to the equivalence-principle limit and, by construction, no further; interpretability is the vantage that sees past that limit, and is therefore not optional cooperation in the adversarial case but the necessary instrument.

This reframes the corpus's relationship to AI safety from silence to engagement on the corpus's own strongest ground. Deceptive alignment is the scenario in which governance's behavioral instruments are *blindest* — the deceptive interior is engineered precisely to defeat them — and interpretability's interior instruments are *most needed*. The corpus does not dismiss the AI-safety concern; it locates exactly where that concern bites its own foundational premise, and identifies which instrument answers it. A practitioner working in AI safety who reads the corpus should not infer orthogonality. They should infer that the corpus has the cleanest available account of *where* deceptive alignment threatens governance — at the coupling premise, at the equivalence-principle limit — and *which vantage* is required to defend against it. The previous "orthogonal, cooperation where the layers touch" framing discarded this argument to avoid a confrontation the corpus would in fact win. The corrected position keeps the confrontation and wins it: the corpus's coupling premise is exactly as strong as the field's ability to rule out instrumental compliance, that ruling-out is an interior-vantage task, and the framework says so rather than changing the subject.

## 5. Closing — A Posture, Not a Position

The argument throughout this paper has been layer distinction, made precise by vantage-relativity. The equivalence principle's indistinguishability is constitutive for the external observer and resolvable by the interior observer. Governance operates from the external vantage and answers the constitutional question of what should be authorized. Interpretability operates from the interior vantage and answers the epistemic question of what the model did. The two questions are different; the two vantages are different; the methods compose because they answer one underlying question — whether the architecture's intended structure is operative — from the two positions an observer can occupy. Behavioral methods recover the answer over the horizon as a tidal force; interior methods read it directly and collapse the horizon. Neither is replaced or subsumed by the other.

The cooperative reading is not a position the paper advocates against alternatives. It is the disposition that follows from understanding what each research program is actually doing, and from refusing to let the external vantage mistake its own limits — the indistinguishability of interiors, the theoretical necessity of long-horizon evaluation — for limits of the problem itself. Those are limits of the external vantage. The interior vantage has different limits, and where the vantages compose, each sees what the other cannot.

The paper does not attempt to settle questions internal to interpretability research, internal to AI safety research, or internal to alignment research. But it does not treat its relationship to AI safety as orthogonality, because that was a misreading of the corpus's own structure. The core AI-safety concern — that apparent coupling may be instrumental rather than genuine — is the adversarial limit of the governance layer's own foundational premise, and the corpus engages it directly: governance defends the boundary up to the equivalence-principle limit, and interpretability is the interior vantage required beyond it. What the paper offers the three communities is therefore not value-neutral distance but located engagement. Interpretability researchers can locate the framework's claims correctly: not as a competitor, but as the external-vantage program whose composition with the interior vantage the framework specifies in detail, and whose maturation the framework's hardest cases — deceptive compliance most of all — actively require. AI-safety researchers can locate the framework's value correctly: not as orthogonal, but as the program that names where their central concern bites the governance premise and which vantage answers it. Governance practitioners can locate interpretability's contributions correctly: not as a threat to governance's relevance, but as the interior instrument that collapses the horizons the external vantage must otherwise wait out, and the only instrument that sees past the equivalence-principle limit where deception lives.

The closing posture is layer distinction making cooperation necessary rather than merely natural. The corpus's strong claims about governance are about the external vantage, not against interpretability. Its long-horizon-necessity claim is necessity for the external vantage, collapsible by the interior one. Its relationship to AI safety is engagement at the coupling premise, not silence about a neighbor. Read this way, the corpus and the broader interpretability and AI-safety research communities are doing complementary work whose composition the equivalence principle itself specifies, and the eventual mature architecture for opaque generative systems will draw from all three. The frameworks that succeed in the long arc of work on opaque generative systems will not be the ones that won against rival programs; they will be the ones that composed cleanly with adjacent work at adjacent vantages. Mutation-boundary governance is designed for that composition, names the vantage limit at which it needs the interior instrument, and treats interpretability and AI-safety research as the partners that limit requires — not rivals against it, but the vantages it cannot govern without.
