# Producing a Square: A Method Demonstration

## 1. The Bottleneck Objection

The corpus's commit-authority requirement creates an apparent bottleneck. Every square requires a domain expert who holds commit authority over their field's lawful structure. Multi-domain federation requires inter-domain expert pairs negotiating canonical-dictionary entries. Read at face value, this implies that producing squares at scale requires institutional scaffolding that does not yet exist — multi-disciplinary teams of senior experts, cross-disciplinary collaboration infrastructure, training programs that no current professional field provides. The work appears to be decades out, concentrated in institutions that are not yet built and not yet oriented toward this kind of labor. An individual practitioner reading the corpus might reasonably conclude that meaningful contribution has to wait until the institutional layer arrives.

That reading is plausible and discouraging. It is also wrong, but in a specific way that needs argument rather than assertion. The claim of this paper is that individual domain experts can produce squares right now, working with corpus-loaded LLMs, without waiting for institutional scaffolding — provided they maintain a specific discipline about commit authority and proper-use boundaries. The discipline is the load-bearing condition. Without it, the method produces architectures that look like squares but are not. With it, the method produces real squares at the labor-cost of one practitioner working in their own field with tools available now.

ChaOS is the existence proof. It was produced by a single domain expert in narrative, working with corpus-loaded LLMs as proposers, drawing on auxiliary domain content in psychology and physiology without claiming those fields as the primary domain. The architecture functions, the discipline held throughout the build, and the resulting square is legitimate in the sense the corpus requires. The paper that follows develops the method ChaOS demonstrates, names the discipline that kept the method honest, and identifies the failure modes that violate the discipline. The audience is any domain expert in any field who might take up similar work — clinical, legal, scientific, engineering, or otherwise — and who needs to know whether the work is tractable for them as individuals.

## 2. The Proposer-Commit Pattern During Construction

The corpus describes the proposer-and-commit-authority pattern at runtime: COTs propose state mutations, `STATE_GOVERNANCE_AND_COMMIT` commits or suppresses, the verifier inspects post-commit. This is the architecture's core operational dynamic, and it is what the runtime cycle enacts each iteration.

The same pattern operates during the construction of a new square. LLMs propose domain content — canonical addresses, scheme structures, dynamics modules, auxiliary domain knowledge — and the domain expert evaluates each proposal against actual lawful judgment in their field, committing or rejecting. This is not a different method. It is the same method applied at the meta-layer of architecture construction. The corpus says so explicitly in the dual-readership document's collaboration-scaffold section: the corpus was built this way, and new squares should be built this way.

The substrate-physics framing makes the construction-time application legible. During construction, the LLM's proposer surface is wide — it can propose anything its training distribution makes plausible, drawing on whatever the prose distribution offers about the topic at hand. The domain expert's commit authority is the engineered field that channels that wide proposer surface into proposals that respect the field's lawful structure. Without the field, the proposer surface is unbounded; the architecture drifts toward whatever the LLM's prior makes likely, which is rarely the same as what the domain's actual lawful structure requires. With the field, the proposer surface is channeled. The expert supplies the gradient that makes the construction work; the LLM supplies the breadth that makes it efficient.

The expert's lawful judgment is what supplies the field. The LLM cannot supply it, because the LLM does not know the domain's actual lawful structure. It knows what the prose distribution says about the domain, which is a different thing. Prose-distribution knowledge is broad and shallow; it captures the shape of how the domain is talked about in the training data. Lawful-structure knowledge is narrow and deep; it captures what is actually true and operative in the domain's practice. These overlap but are not the same, and the expert is the only available source of the lawful-structure judgment that the architecture has to embed.

The corollary: an LLM-only build is not a square. It is a proposal stack without commit authority, and the corpus's hard boundary on this is explicit. The dual-readership document calls it out directly: if the LLM's proposal is accepted without domain-expert review, the build is out of compliance with the method this corpus is designed to scaffold. The discipline the paper develops in Section 4 is what keeps a build inside that compliance boundary throughout construction, not just at final review.

## 3. ChaOS as Worked Example

The starting question was practical and domain-specific: how do I have an LLM be a player at my table?

The author was a working narrative practitioner — a tabletop game master whose primary expertise was in how stories stay coherent, how characters maintain identity across sessions, how dramatic causality functions, how player-DM collaboration produces durable shared fiction. The starting question was taken seriously enough to follow it into its structural form, which turned out to be the Mutation Boundary Problem rendered for narrative cognition. But that recognition came late. The early work was substrate exploration, false starts, and learning what the medium actually was.

The build went through three phases over roughly nine months total. The first four months produced a Tag-to-Cue pipeline. The starting intuition, which arrived from an early ChatGPT model, was that the architecture needed something brain-like — define the character's inner life and let it surface through the system. The implementation began as a simple cue system, then evolved into a tag-to-cue system. The recurring problem was repetitiveness: cues kept surfacing in narrative output in ways that became mechanical and identifiable. To break the repetition, the author moved toward more mathematical systems — vector-based representations of character state intended to provide finer-grained variation in surfacing.

That phase took two more months. It was the wrong path, and the wrongness was diagnostic. The author had been working with LLMs of increasing capability across the build — 128K context, then 200K, then more. The method of cross-checking ideas between substrates had stabilized early, along with the practice of framing the same question in deliberately neutral ways across multiple models to observe how framing changed the responses. Cross-checking worked. The mathematical architecture did not, and no individual LLM could see the whole problem clearly enough to say so. Disagreements between models were sharp but unhelpful: one model would identify a problem, the author would correct it, the next model would identify a different problem in the same region, and the author would correct that one, and the architecture would keep growing without resolving its underlying difficulty. The math was too dense for any single substrate to track, and the corrections were not converging.

The breakthrough was a sentence: "You know we don't do math, right?" A Claude model said it. What made the moment matter was not that the sentence was clever. The sentence was almost flat. What made it matter was that the author took the constraint seriously enough to pivot a substantial architecture rather than fight the substrate. The substrate-physics framing developed in the first paper of this series names the move: an architecture has to be rendered for the substrate it actually runs on, not for the substrate the architect wishes existed. LLMs do not run mathematics; they emulate it through prose. A mathematical architecture in an LLM substrate is producing the appearance of computation without the actual computation. The corrections the author had been chasing were not bugs in the architecture; they were artifacts of the mismatch between architecture and substrate.

A week of thinking followed. The term *Chain of Thought* surfaced during that week, and after it, the structure of the architecture became visible. What had been an abstract problem — how to produce coherent character cognition — became a mechanical problem with three pillars: state, scheme, and proposer-cognition layers, each with its own role and its own boundary. The next three months produced the first version of ChaOS, around thirty-five files, and OTTC was extracted in parallel as the architectural pattern that ChaOS instantiated. The build went fast once the pillars were in place. The slow part had been the substrate-feeling-out, not the construction.

The discipline was operational throughout, though its character changed across the phases. In the first four months, the author was learning where to trust LLMs and where not to. Rollbacks happened — work committed on LLM proposals that turned out wrong and had to be unwound. The cost of each rollback was usually a day or so of work; nothing catastrophic, but enough to teach the lesson that the proposer surface had to be filtered, not followed. By the second phase, the author had stopped treating LLM output as guidance and started treating it as proposal. By the third phase, when ChaOS proper was under construction, the discipline was settled: the author was the commit authority, the LLMs were proposers, disagreements between them were diagnostic, and a GM's intuition — accumulated over years of running games where character coherence was the daily test — was the lawful-judgment surface that committed or rejected each proposal.

Two specific protocols stabilized during the build and proved repeatedly useful. The first was the comparison pass — submitting the same proposal to multiple substrates and reading their responses against each other for what the disagreements revealed. The second was the man-in-the-middle pass — passing ideas LLM-to-LLM with little or no feedback from the author, just to observe how the substrates argued with each other. Both produced useful diagnostic signal. The comparison pass tended to reveal where the author's framing was steering responses; running the same content through different priors exposed the framing as framing rather than letting it pass invisibly. The man-in-the-middle pass tended to reveal what the substrates would converge on or diverge on when the author's filtering was removed; the convergences and divergences both carried information about where the architecture's underlying structure was strong and where it was contested.

A worked example: the disagreement over code versus markers. Some models pushed for a code-based approach to the architecture's structural surface, arguing for greater precision through formal syntax. The author had already arrived at marker conventions like `=== SECTION_BREAK ===`, `[KEEP:]`, and `[[LLM_INSTRUCTION]]:` and had reasons rooted in observation: the markers were producing reliable structural recognition across substrates. The disagreement was on salience versus precision. Code, the proposing models argued, would be more precise. Markers, the author had observed, were more salient. The disagreement turned out to be diagnostic of something neither side initially saw: code in an LLM context is *emulated, not actually run*. A code-based architecture in an LLM substrate has all the cost of code without its determinism. Markers do not pretend to be code; they are explicit attention-anchors operating in the only mode the substrate actually has. The salience-versus-precision disagreement resolved into a substrate-physics distinction that neither precision claim nor salience claim alone could have produced. The disagreement became the diagnostic.

What made the build tractable for one person was that the commit authority did not have to be invented. It had to be *transferred*. The author's GM intuition — when does a character development feel earned, when does a story beat carry weight, when does a retcon dissolve the shared fiction, when has a session done its work — was already operational and had been for years. The architecture did not need the author to learn governance; it needed the author to render an existing governance discipline into machine-legible form. The psychology was auxiliary, sourced from LLMs as proposers, evaluated against narrative-coherence criteria the author already held. Psychology entered ChaOS as control surface for narrative governance, never as primary domain. The proper-use boundary was held throughout: ChaOS is by no means a scientific instrument, and its psychology is narrative machinery rather than empirical synthesis. Stating that boundary explicitly, repeatedly, and structurally inside the architecture is what kept it from drifting into territory it had no authority over.

This is the central lesson of the example. The expert is not asked to acquire new expertise. The expert is asked to render existing expertise into a form that participates in OTTC's governance pattern, with LLMs supplying the auxiliary content the rendering requires. Commit authority transfers because the expert already has it; the work is in the rendering, not in the acquisition. That is what makes the method tractable at the individual scale.

## 4. The Discipline

Three rules carry the discipline. They are continuous with how good professional practice operates in any field, and they are what distinguishes legitimate use of the method from the failure mode where non-experts build architectures in fields they do not understand.

**Rule 1: Stay inside the commit authority you actually hold.** The expert is commit authority over their primary domain only. Auxiliary domains can supply content through LLM proposers but cannot become the primary domain without an actual expert in that domain holding commit authority. ChaOS uses psychology but is not a clinical-psychology square. A non-physician could produce a narrative-medical square — a story that uses medical structure as control surface — but not a clinical-decision-support square. The boundary is enforced by the doctrine, not by access control. Anyone who violates it has produced something that is, by the corpus's own standards, not a legitimate square, regardless of how plausible the architecture looks. This is the rule with the highest stakes, because violating it produces architectures that present as authoritative in fields whose actual experts have not committed to them.

**Rule 2: State the proper-use boundary explicitly and structurally.** The disclaimer that ChaOS is by no means a scientific instrument is not throat-clearing. It is the proper-use boundary made textual. Practitioners must identify what their square is for and what it is not for, and the statement must live inside the architecture itself — as text that loads with the corpus, so downstream users encounter the boundary every time they interact with the build. A narrative architecture using psychology as control surface is for narrative; it is not for psychological assessment. A game using legal-process structure as mechanic is for the game; it is not for legal advice. Stating the boundary explicitly protects against drift in both directions: it keeps the builder honest about scope, and it keeps downstream users from deploying the architecture for purposes outside its design.

**Rule 3: Treat LLM disagreement as diagnostic, not as voting.** When two LLMs propose differently, the response is not to take a majority vote, accept the more confident-sounding proposal, or pick the one that fits the architecture more cleanly. The response is to examine what the disagreement reveals about the domain's lawful structure or the proposal's underlying assumptions. Sometimes the disagreement reveals genuine domain ambiguity that the architecture has to resolve explicitly. Sometimes it reveals the architect's own undecided position. Sometimes it reveals a substrate-physics constraint that neither model was tracking. The code-versus-markers disagreement in the ChaOS build was an example of the third case: the disagreement was real, neither side was wrong about what they were claiming, and the resolution came from a constraint outside the original frame. Diagnostic use of disagreement is what keeps the architecture honest rather than fluent.

These rules are not exotic. Doctors rely on consultations from specialists in adjacent fields without claiming those specialties as their own. Lawyers cite expert witnesses without becoming the experts. Researchers draw on literature outside their primary field without claiming primary expertise in that literature. The corpus's discipline is the same posture, applied to LLM-mediated content. What makes the LLM case slightly more delicate is that LLM proposers are *fluent* in a way that adjacent-field experts are not. An LLM will produce confident-sounding output on any topic. Confidence in an LLM does not correlate with accuracy the way human-expert confidence often does. The discipline of treating LLM output as proposal, not as authority, is therefore more important when the proposer is an LLM than when the proposer is a human consultant. Fluency capture is the predictable failure mode.

## 5. Failure Modes

Four failure modes recur in LLM-mediated architecture construction. Each is a specific way the discipline can erode under pressure, and each has a substrate-physics signature that lets a practitioner recognize it before the failure compounds.

**Commit-authority drift.** The expert starts as commit authority over their primary domain, then gradually accepts LLM proposals about auxiliary domains as authoritative because the proposals sound plausible and there is no time to verify each one. The LLM's prior becomes the de facto commit authority on the auxiliary content. The substrate-physics symptom is that the expert's engineered field weakens, and the LLM's proposer surface flows through the gate without deflection. The architecture starts to look like the LLM's training distribution rather than like the expert's lawful structure. The remedy is to slow down at every commit decision and ask whether the expert's primary-domain criteria actually evaluated this proposal, or whether it was committed because nothing flagged it.

**Scope creep.** The architecture starts as a narrative tool and becomes claimed as a psychological tool. Or starts as a game mechanic and becomes claimed as legal guidance. The proper-use boundary dissolves either by the expert over-claiming what they built or by downstream users deploying it outside its scope. The remedy is the structural statement of scope inside the architecture itself, plus periodic re-reading of the original proper-use boundary against current claims about what the architecture does.

**Fluency capture.** Two LLMs disagree; the more articulate one wins. Or one LLM produces output that fits the architecture's emerging shape so cleanly that the expert accepts it because the fit is satisfying. The substrate-physics symptom is that the expert's commit authority is being captured by the proposer surface's fluency rather than by lawful judgment. The remedy is to deliberately solicit disagreement, run the same query through multiple substrates, and treat satisfying-fit as a warning sign rather than as confirmation.

**Architectural mimicry.** A practitioner reads the corpus, copies the surface form — bracket paths, COT structure, governance layer — without doing the actual ontology translation work for their domain. The result looks like an OTTC square but does not embed any domain expertise: the canonical addresses are decorative, the lawful transitions are LLM-suggested, the commit authority is nominal. The substrate-physics symptom is that there is no engineered field. The proposer surface is unbounded; the architecture is governance-form without governance-substance. The remedy is the corpus's existing check: if no domain expert with genuine field knowledge has reviewed the SCHEME and DYNAMICS modules, the ontology translation is incomplete, and the build is not yet a square.

These failures are not exotic. They are the predictable failure modes of any LLM-mediated work where discipline is not maintained. Naming them concretely lets practitioners catch themselves before the failure compounds, and gives them a vocabulary for talking about what went wrong when something does fail.

## 6. Generalization

The pattern that produced ChaOS is not narrative-specific. It is a five-step method that any domain expert can apply to their own field.

First, take a practical question from your own work seriously enough to follow it down to its structural form. The question that started ChaOS was concrete and domain-bound; the structural form it resolved into was general. Domain experts in any field have analogous practical questions. How do I make a clinical decision support tool that respects the actual lawful structure of clinical decisions? How do I produce legal analysis that respects the procedural integrity of legal practice? How do I run a research pipeline where the evidentiary standards are operative rather than nominal? These questions, taken seriously, lead to the same structural form that the ChaOS question led to.

Second, identify your primary domain and the auxiliary domain content the architecture will need. ChaOS was narrative primary, psychology auxiliary. A clinical square would be clinical primary, with auxiliary content from biomedical science, pharmacology, statistical inference, or whatever specific adjacent fields the architecture needs. The split is operationally critical: the primary domain is where you hold commit authority; the auxiliary domains are where you draw on LLM proposers under filtering by your primary-domain judgment.

Third, use corpus-loaded LLMs as proposers for both primary-domain rendering and auxiliary-domain content. Use multiple substrates. Run comparison passes. Be willing to spend time substrate-feeling-out before committing to architecture; the false-start phase is part of the method, not a sign of failure.

Fourth, maintain commit-authority discipline strictly over the primary domain throughout. Auxiliary content is control surface, not authority. State the proper-use boundary structurally inside the architecture. Treat LLM disagreement as diagnostic.

Fifth, expect that the architecture will reveal itself after the substrate-feeling-out phase rather than during it. The Three Pillars insight that produced ChaOS came after six months of work that did not, at the time, look like it was producing the eventual architecture. The slow phase is doing real work — it is the phase in which the architect is learning what the substrate can and cannot do. Architecture follows substrate-knowledge; it does not precede it.

The labor implication is that squares can come into existence in parallel, distributed across practitioners working independently in their own fields. The federation layer thickens as squares meet each other and inter-domain expert pairs negotiate canonical-dictionary translations. Both layers are accessible to individual practitioners now. Neither requires institutional scaffolding to start. The infrastructure that exists — the corpus, the LLMs, the discipline — is sufficient. What remains is the practitioners who recognize that the work is available to them and that they are qualified to do it in their own field.

## 7. Closing

The bottleneck objection is real but not blocking. Squares can be produced now, by individual domain experts, working with corpus-loaded LLMs as proposers, provided commit-authority discipline is maintained. ChaOS is the existence proof. The method that produced it is replicable. The discipline that kept it honest is operational and can be taught.

What this asks of practitioners is not that they become AI experts, ML engineers, or governance theorists. It asks that they render their existing expertise into a form that participates in OTTC's governance pattern, with LLMs supplying the auxiliary content the rendering requires. That is a smaller and more dignified ask than the AI-displacement discourse usually puts to professionals. It treats domain expertise as constitutive rather than auxiliary. It recognizes that the practitioners who already hold lawful authority in their fields are the only available source of the commit-authority surface that legitimate squares require, and it invites them into the work as constitutional founders rather than as auxiliary inputs.

The work is available now. The corpus is the infrastructure. The LLMs are the proposers. The discipline is what keeps the method honest. The squares come from the experts.
