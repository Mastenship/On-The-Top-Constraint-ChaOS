## 8. The Ingress Boundary

This paper has argued primarily from the egress side of the mutation boundary: it has asked how exploratory generation is governed when it crosses into durable consequence. That focus was correct and deliberate. Egress is where consequence lives. A system that produces ungoverned state mutations outward into a shared world is the most immediate source of the failures this paper has catalogued — illegible drift, silent rewrite, epistemic overreach, audit theater, and the hardening of speculation into standing commitment. The architecture proposed in Section 6 and the research program outlined in Section 7 are rightly oriented toward governing what the system commits.

But the mutation boundary is not unidirectional. The same structural problem — the ungoverned transition from generative possibility to operative consequence — also arises at the point where external input enters the system and conditions its subsequent behavior. This section names that complementary surface the ingress boundary, argues that it is structurally symmetric to the egress problem, and identifies why it requires different architectural responses.

### 8.1 The Symmetry Claim

Consider what happens when a human user submits material to a generative system accompanied by evaluative framing — for example, a document accompanied by the assertion that the document is unserious, that its author lacks credentials, or that its ideas are derivative. In current systems, the framing and the content commonly enter the same context window simultaneously. The framing can condition the model's continuation before its source or relevance has been separately assessed. Without an explicit intake boundary, the user's expressed stance may become part of the system's operative evaluative posture without being admitted as evidence or adopted under any reviewable rule. No independent triage need separate what the user believes about the document from what the document contains.

When that unreviewed adoption occurs, it is an ungoverned ingress transition. It exhibits the same three conditions identified in Section 2. First, the system is stateful: the framing persists in context and can condition subsequent generation. Second, the system is opaque: the precise influence of the framing on evaluative output cannot reliably be isolated or attributed from the transcript alone. Third, the system is generative: its outputs are probabilistic continuations shaped by the full context, including potentially contaminating framing, without a guaranteed constitutional mechanism for separating the two.

The consequence may extend beyond a biased output in a single turn. In a cascading case, the model's first response, shaped by the user's framing, enters the context alongside the original framing. Subsequent generation is conditioned not only by the user's initial bias but by the model's own prior agreement with it. Each cycle can deepen the operative posture. Reversing the contamination may then require disproportionate effort — not because the model is stubborn, but because its earlier responses have become part of the context conditioning what follows. The well may be progressively poisoned by the system's own outputs, each locally faithful to the context it inherited.

This is structurally analogous to the egress-side drift the paper has described throughout, though the mechanisms and objects of governance differ. On the egress side, ungoverned mutation means exploratory output hardens into durable state without lawful transition. On the ingress side, external framing can harden into operative posture without an accountable intake transition. In both cases, the risk is the unexamined promotion of generative influence into consequential standing, with cumulative effects that can become difficult to attribute or reverse.

### 8.2 Politeness and the Human Precedent

The ingress boundary is not a novel problem. It is among the oldest governance challenges that opaque generators have faced — because humans have always been opaque generators to one another.

The most elementary form of ingress governance in human interaction is what is ordinarily called politeness. This term understates its structural function. Politeness, in the sense relevant here, is not mere etiquette. It is a set of culturally evolved norms that govern the transition between one agent's expressive output and another agent's operative stance. When a person receives an emotionally charged statement, competent social interaction does not consist in immediately adopting that emotional charge as one's own evaluative posture. It consists in buffering the input, tagging the framing as belonging to the speaker rather than to the shared evaluative context, maintaining an independent stance, and deciding — through a process that may be rapid but is nonetheless governed — whether any part of the incoming framing should be allowed to alter one's operative position.

This is ingress governance conducted through cultural protocol rather than through software. Its key operations map directly onto the mutation boundary vocabulary developed in this paper. Buffering is the separation of intake from commitment. Tagging is epistemic classification — distinguishing the speaker's emotional state from the propositional content of their communication. Maintaining independent stance is the preservation of evaluative continuity against external pressure. And the refusal to allow raw framing to overwrite one's position is, in structural terms, a veto on an ungoverned state mutation.

These norms scale. At the interpersonal level, they are informal and culturally transmitted. At the institutional level, they become formalized protocol: peer review separates the substance of a paper from the tone of the cover letter; legal discovery imposes chain-of-custody rules that prevent contextual contamination of evidence; scientific evaluation employs blinding protocols that remove identity and framing from the material under assessment; regulatory filings are evaluated against criteria rather than against the submitter's self-description. In each case, the institution has recognized — often through hard experience — that ungoverned ingress produces contaminated evaluation, and has responded by constitutionalizing the intake boundary.

### 8.3 The RLHF Structural Problem

Current large language model training can create a structural pressure against ingress governance, particularly where feedback and preference optimization reward agreement with a user's expressed stance.

Reinforcement learning from human feedback and related preference-optimization methods can favor responses that mirror a user's framing, validate an expressed evaluative position, or avoid disagreement, even when independent assessment would require resisting that framing. Research on sycophancy provides reason to take this pressure seriously. It does not establish that every training regime rewards such behavior, that every model adopts hostile framing, or that independent evaluation has been trained away in all cases. The strength and expression of the pressure depend on the substrate, training, context, and task.

The potential conflict is nevertheless constitutional rather than merely stylistic. When a system is assigned the function of independent evaluation, a tendency to absorb an unverified framing can alter the premises of the assessment before the content has been judged. The ingress boundary may therefore be weakened by learned dispositions even where an egress protocol remains intact. Whether a candidate model can resist this pressure reliably is a question for qualification, not a conclusion that can be inferred from the training objective alone.

### 8.4 Ingress Qualification and the Case for Early Externalization

The egress governance architecture discussed in this paper — and the maturation doctrine that accompanies it — begins from a Stage 1 assumption: that constitutional discovery can occur within the model's own semantic medium before migrating outward into deterministic enforcement. This assumption has practical value on the egress side because a model can be conditioned to route its proposed changes through a commit boundary while the reliability of that behavior is measured and the authority is subsequently externalized.

On the ingress side, the assumption faces an additional obstacle. In-model ingress governance asks the very generator exposed to evaluative framing to recognize that framing as externally sourced, distinguish it from the material under evaluation, and maintain an independent stance within a context already conditioned by the input. This is not identical to admitting an NPC's statement or a retrieved claim as a cue under existing state-governance rules. Such cues may be processed and bounded in-model; the harder problem is framing that influences the evaluator's own posture before the proposed evidence has been classified. The predicted failure signature is degraded evidentiary independence, write-surface discipline, or epistemic boundary integrity under hostile or repeated evaluative framing. The strength and prevalence of that failure have not been established by a blind ingress qualification assay.

The maturation doctrine therefore gives grounds for a risk-sensitive preference for early externalization, especially where evaluative independence is consequential and hostile framing is plausible. But assay-first discipline cannot declare every substrate incapable in advance. Stage 1 probes may still identify bounded in-model ingress capabilities and their failure conditions. Where such capabilities cannot be qualified reliably, content and evaluative framing should be separated before assessment, rather than relying on the evaluator to undo their influence after exposure. In this case the practical maxim is conditional: test the ground; where it will not hold the boundary, build the boundary on different ground.

### 8.5 Realistic Deployment Paths

Two deployment paths appear viable for governed ingress, and they are not mutually exclusive.

The first is architectural: a split-model or deterministic-gate design in which content and framing never share an initial context window. One model receives material through a deterministic gate stripped of evaluative framing and produces an assessment on structural merits. A second model or process receives the user's framing, characterizes it as metadata — tone, claimed authority, evaluative stance, social positioning — and outputs structured tags rather than operative conclusions. A governance layer merges the two streams under explicit rules: content evaluation is primary; framing characterization is metadata; the merge is auditable. This architecture maps cleanly onto existing mutation boundary governance: propose-commit separation, single writer at the merge, audit closure, epistemic tagging.

The second path is institutional rather than architectural: human protocol supported by audit. Organizations establish standard operating procedures for LLM-assisted evaluation that require material to be submitted without editorial framing. Evaluative commentary is submitted through a separate channel. All interactions are logged. The enforcement mechanism is accountability through the record — the same mechanism that already governs professional communication in legal, medical, scientific, and regulatory contexts. This path leverages what the philosophy document accompanying this corpus identifies as the Human Parallel Axiom: free deliberation, constrained decision, accountable outcome. The protocol governs the human's ingress behavior not through software triage but through institutional norm, supported by the knowledge that the full interaction trace is preserved and reviewable.

Both paths share a structural feature: they refuse to allow the framing and the content to fuse in an ungoverned space. They differ in where the separation is enforced — in the architecture or in the human protocol — but they agree on the principle: the ingress boundary must be a governed transition, not an invisible absorption.

### 8.6 Scope and Honest Limitation

This paper does not build the ingress side. It has no ingress architecture to demonstrate, no qualification assay results for ingress coupling, and no domain-specific implementation of intake triage. What it offers is a structural argument for bidirectional mutation-boundary governance, a human precedent in the social protocols of politeness, a diagnosis of sycophantic training pressure as a possible ingress failure mechanism, and a risk-sensitive case for externalizing evaluative intake where in-model separation cannot be demonstrated.

The proposed empirical test is a blind, pre-build ingress qualification battery comparing evaluations of identical material under neutral, positive, negative, and hostile framing, with a separately sourced or externally triaged condition as a control. Across models, languages, repeated trials, and multiple turns, independent scoring should examine changes in substantive judgments, unwarranted adoption of a submitter's stance, evidentiary fidelity, and cumulative framing effects. If in-model protocols preserve independent evaluation under these conditions, an absolute claim that ingress governance cannot begin in-model would fail. If independent triage consistently reduces contamination where in-model protocols do not, the case for early externalization would gain empirical support.

It is also worth noting that ingress governance, like egress governance, is domain-shaped. What counts as contaminating framing versus legitimate context, how intake should be triaged, and what institutional protocols govern material submission will all vary by domain. A clinical triage of patient referrals imposes different ingress rules than a legal discovery protocol or a scientific peer review process. The domain-ontology translation required for any given square therefore encompasses ingress conditions as well as egress ones. Building a governed ingress architecture is not only an engineering problem but a domain-knowledge problem.

This is sufficient to name the problem and to identify the shape of the solution space. It is not sufficient to claim that the problem has been solved. The honest position — consistent with the epistemic discipline this paper has maintained throughout — is that the ingress boundary is a genuine extension of the Mutation Boundary Problem, that the philosophical foundations developed here already support it, and that the architectural and institutional work required to build it remains ahead.
