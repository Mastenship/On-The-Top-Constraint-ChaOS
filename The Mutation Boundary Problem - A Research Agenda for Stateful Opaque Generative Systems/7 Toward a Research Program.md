## 7. Toward a Research Program

If the Mutation Boundary Problem is to become more than a descriptive insight, it must be turned into a comparative and testable research program. That requires moving beyond general intuitions about governance, continuity, or legibility and asking what can actually be studied across architectures, domains, languages, and model classes. The purpose of this section is therefore not to outline one canonical experimental design, but to outline the kinds of empirical and conceptual work needed to make progress. The broad claim is simple: if stateful opaque generative systems are to be trusted as participants in long-horizon settings, then evaluation must shift from output quality alone toward the governance quality of durable change.

A note on this section's relationship to Section 4 is warranted. Section 4 decomposed the Mutation Boundary Problem into eleven question clusters that map the conceptual terrain of the problem. The research priorities that follow do not stand in one-to-one correspondence with those clusters. They are ordered and aggregated differently because the criterion is now research tractability rather than conceptual completeness. Some clusters split across multiple priorities because their tractable sub-questions warrant separate treatment. Some clusters merge with adjacent ones because their research methods overlap. Some priorities draw from clusters that did not appear as such in Section 4 because the research framing surfaces them more cleanly than the conceptual decomposition did. Readers using this paper as a reference document should expect Section 4 to map the territory and Section 7 to identify the workable next steps within that territory; the two structures are complementary rather than parallel.

A first priority is to distinguish unit qualification from system regression. Many current workflows test only the performance of a built system and then infer backwards that the underlying model is fit for the role it has been assigned. OTTC's assay-first doctrine suggests a different discipline that is worth investigating more broadly. Before a machine or ontology is built around a candidate model, the model should be probed in a fresh and minimally conditioned setting to determine whether it can reliably inhabit the intended constitutional interface at all. This includes, at minimum, testing canonical address obedience, write-surface discipline, epistemic boundary integrity where applicable, and audit non-theater. Such qualification results should then inform role assignment, authority scope, and externalization depth. Post-build retesting remains necessary, but it answers a different question: whether the engineered system has preserved or degraded invariants after design choices were made. Research that conflates these two stages risks misclassifying conditioned compliance as underlying coupling strength.

A second priority is the development of mutation-boundary metrics, and it is useful to divide these into two classes that measure different things at different timescales. The first class is per-cycle and per-violation: measures that capture whether each transition, taken on its own, was lawful. The second class is longitudinal: measures that capture whether a sequence of individually lawful transitions is collectively well-behaved over the horizon. Standard evaluations of generative systems often focus on accuracy, task completion, latency, safety refusals, or user satisfaction. These are useful but insufficient for the present problem, and even the per-cycle mutation-boundary measures, while necessary, do not exhaust it.

The per-cycle class includes canonical address compliance, rate of illegal direct-write attempts, audit coverage correctness, state-thrash frequency, epistemic boundary violation rate, verification coherence, and replayability of state deltas under repeated scenario batteries. These capture the integrity of individual transitions and the reproducibility of the commit record. They are the measures most readily derived from existing evaluation practice, and they detect the failure modes that announce themselves at the moment they occur: a direct write, a dropped proposal, an oscillating dial, a committed-fact claim without evidence.

The longitudinal class is less obvious and, on the argument of Section 2.1, more fundamental, because the deepest failure the framework predicts does not announce itself at any single cycle. If ungoverned drift is thermalization — the directional relaxation of a maintained low-entropy partition toward disorder once boundary work becomes insufficient — then its signature is an entropy-production rate: a directional, accumulating bias in committed state across many cycles, composed entirely of individually lawful steps. No per-cycle metric can detect this, because no per-cycle transition violates a rule; the disorder is in the cumulative direction, not in any single move. State-thrash frequency, the nearest per-cycle relative, detects oscillation — competing rewrites visible within a cycle — which is a categorically different failure from slow directional creep. The required measure is therefore a biased-governed-drift rate: the degree to which the committed time-series of a given durable field trends in one direction beyond what the accumulated cue evidence justifies, distinguished from unbiased fluctuation around a justified value. This is the dependent variable the thermodynamic argument names and the per-cycle metric set cannot supply.

The instrument this measurement requires is a downstream semantic-to-numeric layer that encodes committed semantic deltas after audit and verification and compares them across cycles for directional bias — a longitudinal calorimeter for the boundary, measuring the rate at which order is being lost rather than whether any single transition was legal. The corpus's semantic-abacus discipline, in which canonical semantic tokens remain the model-facing write surface while numeric encoding and cross-cycle comparison are performed in deterministic downstream governance, is the natural home for this instrument: the semantic tokens are the beads, and the entropy-production rate is computed over the committed bead-sequence, not at the model's write surface. This also resolves a tension the framework would otherwise carry unaddressed. Section 5 and Section 6.2 argue that governed variance is productive in the narrative domain — character rather than defect — while Section 2.1 argues that accumulated variance is the deepest failure. These are reconciled only by the longitudinal measurement: per-cycle governed fluctuation without net bias is productive variance, and directionally biased governed fluctuation is thermalization, and the entropy-production rate is the quantity that separates them. Domain-specific additions will still be needed at both timescales. Narrative systems may require blinded human ratings of continuity, bounded subjectivity, and player-likeness alongside the drift-rate measure. Scientific or analytic systems may require per-cycle rates of unsupported hypothesis hardening and evidence-request discipline, together with longitudinal measures of whether a body of committed conclusions is drifting directionally under repeated exposure. The key point is that mutation quality must become measurable as its own object, and that measurability has both a per-cycle and a longitudinal axis, the latter being the one the thermodynamic structure of the boundary makes indispensable.

A third research direction concerns comparative constitutional architectures. OTTC is one proposed response to the mutation boundary, but it should not be treated as the only candidate family. The field would benefit from systematic comparisons among systems with different governance shapes: free-form memory architectures, planner-validator pipelines, retrieval-heavy agents, schema-first deterministic wrappers, and propose-commit constitutional systems. Such comparisons should ask not only which architecture performs better on local tasks, but which maintains more legible and bounded state evolution over long horizons. The entropy-production rate introduced above gives this comparison a substrate-neutral dependent variable: an architecture maintains order to the degree that its committed state does not thermalize under extended operation, and competing architectures can be ranked by the drift rate they exhibit under matched scenario batteries. In this respect, the Mutation Boundary Problem may help reframe comparison itself. Rather than asking only which system is "smarter," the field can ask which system remains more governable — which is to say, which system loses order more slowly — as persistence, consequence, and time increase.

A fourth priority is language-conditioned coupling research. Because governance in these systems is partly language-native, coupling strength should not be assumed to transfer cleanly across languages, cultures, or rhetorical surfaces. The same constitution may hold strongly in one language and weakly in another. Canonical token discipline, epistemic modesty, deference to operation vocabularies, and tolerance for ambiguity may all vary across model family and operating language. This has important implications for multilingual or globally deployed systems. Research should therefore investigate not only whether a constitutional interface works, but where and under what language conditions it works. Such work may influence canonical lexicon design, translation-before-commit policies, and the placement of deterministic boundaries in multilingual settings.

A fifth line of work concerns the relation between semantic governance and deterministic enforcement. One of the central claims emerging from OTTC is that semantic distinctions must often be discovered in-model before they can be safely externalized. But this claim itself should be tested, refined, and perhaps bounded. Under what conditions can a schema-first design succeed without assay-first discovery? What kinds of semantic distinctions survive externalization cleanly, and which ones collapse when moved too early into rigid software? How much of a governance protocol must be inhabited by the model itself before deterministic systems can meaningfully enforce it? Conversely, at what point does remaining too long in pure in-model governance accumulate unacceptable risk? These questions are architectural and developmental rather than purely performance-based, and they are likely to differ by domain and consequence level.

Related to this is the need for research on maturation pathways. OTTC proposes a Stage 1 / Stage 2 / Stage 3 developmental doctrine: semantic discovery, hybrid migration, deterministic enforcement. This sequence is plausible, but it should be treated as an empirical claim about engineering practice rather than a self-validating narrative. Comparative studies could examine systems that begin with fully externalized state, systems that remain in-context, and systems that migrate gradually. Relevant outcomes might include parser brittleness, schema drift, authority confusion, governance debt, and cost of redesign after failure. A specific and underexamined risk deserves identification within this priority: verifier substitution. Governance patterns discovered in a Stage 1 environment that includes a strong human verifier — the game master in the narrative case — may have silently offloaded part of the governance burden onto that verifier, catching failure modes in real time without ever encoding them into the architecture. When the protocol is externalized and the human verifier is replaced by a deterministic one, the work the human was doing invisibly becomes visible, and may not survive the substitution. What migrates badly may therefore be not the schema but the unstated dependence on a verifier the target domain does not possess. Research on maturation should ask, for any candidate pattern, what work the discovery-stage verifier was performing, whether that work was encoded or merely absorbed, and whether the externalized verifier can reproduce it. This question is structurally identical, at the egress boundary, to the diagnosis Section 8 develops at the ingress boundary, where the work a competent human performs invisibly becomes expensive and explicit the moment one attempts to build its machine equivalent.

A sixth research area is tool mediation as evidence governance. Once tools enter the picture, the mutation boundary no longer concerns only internal state. It also concerns requests for information, calculations, API calls, and real-world actions. The field needs a better understanding of when a system should be allowed to infer, when it should be required to request evidence, and how tool requests should themselves be represented and governed. One promising framing is to treat tool calls not merely as execution actions but as auditable evidence motions: proposals that ask a governance layer to authorize a measurement, retrieval, or actuation. Research here could compare direct tool-calling agents against mediated architectures that separate proposal, authorization, execution, and reintegration. Relevant metrics might include overuse, underuse, provenance quality, calibration shift after retrieval, and the impact of external evidence on later state mutation.

A seventh priority is error taxonomy and tolerance research. The paper has argued that different domains assign different meanings to error, and that some domains may tolerate or even productively integrate certain legible deviations while others may not. This suggests the need for a more explicit typology. Which errors are purely constitutional — for example, illegal writes, audit omissions, or verification incoherence? Which are epistemic — for example, unsupported certainty or scope violations? Which are domain-relative — for example, scientifically unacceptable but narratively usable distortions? And cutting across all three, which errors are per-cycle and which are longitudinal — the distinction the thermodynamic argument makes central, between a single unlawful transition and a sequence of lawful transitions that drifts directionally? Under what conditions can governed error remain value-bearing rather than destructive? These questions matter because architectures should not be judged by a single undifferentiated standard of correctness where domain conditions differ fundamentally, and because the timescale at which an error is detectable determines what instrument is needed to detect it at all.

An eighth research direction concerns multi-agent constitutional form. Many future systems will not consist of a single generator acting alone. They will involve multiple models, humans, tools, and external processes sharing some form of state or consequence space. This raises questions about how constitutional order should scale. Can multiple proposing agents safely share one commit authority? When do overlapping ontologies require a common canonical dictionary? How should authority be partitioned across citizen, governor, and auditor roles? What forms of conflict resolution remain intelligible when more than one generator contributes to a shared world? Research here may benefit from drawing not only on computer science and AI safety, but also on organizational theory, institutional design, and jurisprudence, since the problem becomes increasingly political as shared state becomes more socially consequential.

A ninth and especially important line of inquiry concerns domain-specific success metrics. One of the major risks in this area is evaluating all systems as though they serve the same end. They do not. A narrative character system is not successful for the same reasons a scientific analysis assistant is successful. An enterprise workflow governor is not successful for the same reasons a roleplay participant is successful. The Mutation Boundary Problem does not erase these differences; it provides a way to compare them without collapsing them. Future work should therefore be explicit about what counts as success in each domain. For narrative systems, this may include continuity, emotional legibility, bounded point of view, and meaningful transformation. For analytical systems, it may include evidence discipline, provenance quality, and explicit uncertainty handling. For high-stakes domains, it may include suppression of unauthorized mutation and the reliability of deterministic oversight. Without such explicitness, governance architectures risk being overgeneralized or misapplied.

A tenth research direction — domain-expert constitutional discovery — deserves explicit identification. The analysis in this paper implies that any new OTTC-governed architecture requires prior ontology translation: the domain expert must render their field's constitutional world (durable things, lawful transitions, evidentiary standards, authority assignments) into OTTC-legible form before governance can operate. But the method by which this translation happens is itself understudied. This paper's corpus is designed to function as a machine-facing collaboration scaffold: when loaded into persistent LLM context, it allows LLMs to assist domain experts in articulating ontology surfaces, proposing canonical addresses, identifying gaps in lawful mutation coverage, and validating architectural proposals against the rectangle invariants. The domain expert retains commit authority; the LLM proposes; disagreements between model proposals or between model and expert serve as diagnostic signals rather than obstacles. Research questions here include: what makes a domain's ontology tractable for OTTC translation? What forms of collaboration are most productive? What failure modes arise when domain knowledge is insufficiently translated before governance architecture is built? How should the discovery process be scaffolded across different domain types? These questions matter because OTTC's reach as a governance pattern depends directly on how well domain experts can build new squares, and that in turn depends on the quality of the translation and discovery method.

Finally, the research program must remain open to the possibility that current proposals are incomplete, local, or wrong in ways not yet visible. The Mutation Boundary Problem itself is an attempt to name a space of questions more clearly, not to declare those questions closed. This means the field should actively seek falsification conditions. Under what circumstances does a propose-commit architecture fail to improve legibility? When does canonicalization become theater rather than coupling? When does audit collapse into performative compliance? When does a constitutional interface overfit one model family and fail to generalize? When does a domain's success criterion reveal that a given governance shape is solving the wrong problem? And specifically for the thermodynamic framing introduced in this paper: does the predicted entropy-production signature actually appear — do ungoverned and weakly governed systems exhibit measurable directional drift in committed state that well-governed systems do not, under matched long-horizon batteries? If the signature does not appear where the theory predicts it, the thermodynamic framing is decorative rather than load-bearing, and should be demoted accordingly. These are not weaknesses in the research agenda; they are signs that the agenda is treating itself seriously.

The immediate value of this program is practical as well as conceptual. By shifting attention from output alone to the quality of governed state transition — and, crucially, by adding a longitudinal axis on which the slow loss of order becomes measurable rather than invisible — it offers a way to compare systems that might otherwise seem incomparable. It suggests new metrics, new assay practices, new architectural questions, and new distinctions between semantic and deterministic authority. It also opens a path for domains that are currently poorly served by standard evaluation regimes, including narrative participation, long-horizon collaborative agents, and other systems in which continuity matters as much as local correctness. The next and final section returns to the central claim of the paper: if opaque generative systems are to matter across time, then the mutation boundary must become a first-class object of design, evaluation, and governance.
