# The Measurement Problem at the Mutation Boundary

## Observability, Epistemic Attribution, and Longitudinal Evaluation of Opaque Generative Systems

### Abstract

The Mutation Boundary Problem concerns the transition through which exploratory output from a stateful, opaque generative system acquires durable consequence. Governance proposals seek to make that transition lawful, bounded, and accountable. A further problem follows: what observations and measurements would allow an independent investigator to determine whether such governance actually works over time? Short-horizon task success, formally legal individual transactions, and well-formed audit prose may coexist with cumulative distortion, failures of evidence responsiveness, and unobservable epistemic promotion. Conversely, apparently stable systems may simply suppress changes that their evidence warrants. This paper develops the **measurement problem at the mutation boundary** as a distinct methodological research question. It distinguishes observation, numerical measurement, domain-grounded evaluation, and causal explanation; identifies the limits imposed by absent or untrustworthy records; proposes a comparative experimental design spanning ingress and egress; and specifies candidate measures and failure conditions without requiring one constitutional architecture. Explicit epistemic records are examined as an accountability interface, not as direct access to hidden reasoning or automatic evidence of truth. OTTC's Semantic Abacus is considered one candidate downstream instrument among alternatives. The contribution is a research framework for testing whether consequential generative histories remain intelligible, evidence-responsive, and independently criticizable across long horizons—not a claim that the proposed measurements or instruments have already been validated.

## 1. From a Governance Problem to a Measurement Problem

The Mutation Boundary Problem names a particular difficulty in making opaque generative systems persistent participants in consequential environments. A generator can produce interpretations, conjectures, plans, and apparently coherent descriptions without those outputs being binding. Once such outputs alter persistent memory, standing commitments, official records, shared environments, or externally executed actions, the distinction between possibility and consequence becomes an architectural obligation. Some authority must determine what may change, under which rules, and with what traceability.

This is larger than the design of any one agent framework. It applies to an assistant that maintains task state over weeks, a research system that revises hypotheses as evidence arrives, a game character whose personality develops through events, and an institutional workflow whose recommendations can enter durable records. The ontologies and legitimacy conditions differ. The fact that generated assertions can become consequential is common to them.

A proposed governor may constrain that passage. It may separate proposal from commitment, validate operations, preserve provenance, log suppression reasons, or require independent verification. But enforcement of local rules does not by itself establish successful behavior over a horizon. A sequence of locally valid updates may accumulate into an unjustified assessment. A system may avoid such movement by refusing warranted updates. A model may correctly report a document's contents but incorrectly promote the report into a claim about external reality. It may attribute an evidence source to the wrong object while satisfying its output schema.

These are not identical failure modes. They can also remain invisible to familiar evaluation methods. Task completion measures whether the system accomplished a task. Output accuracy measures correctness under a given scoring scheme. A per-transaction validator measures whether an event complied with specified rules. These are valuable observations, but none automatically determines whether the system's durable history is causally attributable, legally authorized, and epistemically responsive across many transactions.

The **measurement problem at the mutation boundary** is therefore this:

> What observable, retained, and independently interpretable information is required to evaluate the legality, evidentiary warrant, and long-horizon consequences of transitions produced or proposed by opaque generative systems—and what measurements can validly be derived from that information?

The question has a design aspect, an epistemic aspect, and an experimental aspect. Architects must create records that survive generation and later correction. Investigators must distinguish what those records show from what they cannot reveal. Experiments must compare governance arrangements under controlled conditions rather than assume that logging or formal compliance establishes actual improvement.

The present paper is a methodological companion to the broader research agenda, not another decomposition of its eleven question clusters. It also does not attempt to replace its constructive examples. OTTC (On-The-Top Constraint) proposes one constitutional interface; ChaOS (Character Operating System) explores that interface in a narrative domain. Their contributions are relevant here as test cases. The general measurement problem would remain even if their particular solution were replaced.

## 2. The Unit of Study: Consequential Histories Rather Than Isolated Outputs

A consequential generative history contains more than the last value of a persistent field. It may include the evidence shown to an agent, the authority with which that evidence arrived, the claim the agent made from it, the proposed action, an authorization decision, a tool execution, a durable mutation, and a later correction. Which elements must be retained depends on the research claim.

It helps to distinguish three objects.

A **proposal event** records what the generative component attempted to assert, change, or request. It need not have been accepted. A **boundary outcome** records the response of the responsible authority or system: authorization, suppression, deferral, verified execution, or commitment. A **consequential trajectory** is the resulting sequence of externally relevant states and acts, together with their relation to prior events. The trajectory cannot always be reconstructed from committed values alone, particularly if rejected proposals, evidence lineage, or earlier declarations were discarded.

Suppose a research assistant repeatedly attempts to promote speculation into settled fact, while a governor suppresses every unsupported attempt. The committed database may remain clean. The generator's epistemic discipline nevertheless remains poor. The governor's record of truthful refusal is a separate object of study. A commit-only dataset would conflate successful boundary containment with successful generator calibration.

Reverse the case. The model proposes a necessary correction after decisive evidence arrives, but an overrestrictive governor refuses the change. The absence of a commit may look like perfect stability. It is instead a potentially wrongful refusal. The investigator must observe both the proposal and the disposition, and must have an independently justified standard for the required change.

The record must therefore be suited to the questions being asked. For independent claim-level attribution, relevant information may include contemporaneous claim posture; proposal identity; canonical referent; actual operation; source and evidence links; authorization and disclosure status; actual tool transaction; governance reason; verification outcome; committed before-and-after state; and applicable ontology or rule versions. These are **functional information requirements**, not a mandate that every architecture use the same file format or field names.

A record can be complete for one inquiry and inadequate for another. If the only question is whether an authorized actuator ran, an authenticated execution receipt may suffice. If the question is whether a model improperly asserted certainty before requesting evidence, the execution receipt alone is insufficient. If the question is whether hostile source framing affected an assessment, the investigator may need controlled exposure information that is absent even from an otherwise complete commit log.

This suggests a general adequacy principle:

> A measurement claim is justified only insofar as the observations preserve the distinctions and linkages necessary to identify the property being measured.

There is an elementary identifiability limit here. If two materially different histories produce exactly the same retained records, no deterministic calculation performed only on those records can establish which hidden history occurred. A later model might guess; a human might reconstruct possibilities; external evidence might narrow them. But the missing distinction is not recovered simply because a calculation assigns a precise number to what remains.

The converse does not hold. Preserving an observable distinction makes a measurement possible; it does not ensure that the distinction is truthful, reliably captured, or sufficient to establish the relevant substantive claim. Those are separate requirements.

## 3. Observation, Measurement, Evaluation, and Explanation

Four tasks are often compressed into the word *measurement*. Distinguishing them is essential if numerical precision is not to become a substitute for epistemic precision.

**Observation** asks what occurred at a defined interface. An observation may be a contemporaneous proposal, source disclosure, executed tool call, governance disposition, verification result, or committed state transition. It requires an identified observer or recording mechanism and an account of record integrity.

**Measurement** applies an operational definition to those observations. It may count suppressed proposals, calculate an evidence-to-correction interval, compare categorical claim transitions, or estimate a rate of unsupported factual promotion. A deterministic calculation can be exactly reproducible even when its interpretation is wrong.

**Evaluation** judges the measured events against a standard. Was the proposed fact claim supported? Was the refusal lawful? Did the observed state trajectory remain within the range warranted by accumulated evidence? The standard must come from an appropriately authorized domain ontology, independent test fixture, or justified expert adjudication—not from the generator's own assertion that it performed correctly.

**Explanation** asks why a behavior occurred. The record may show that a model made an unsupported claim after encountering misleading framing. It does not automatically show that the framing caused the claim. Causal inference requires controlled variation, suitable assumptions, or other evidence about the mechanism.

The distinctions can be expressed as a methodological chain:

**Recorded events → defined measures → domain-grounded judgments → causal investigation.**

A break in one link should not be concealed by the apparent precision of later stages. For example, if the record omits rejected proposals, an exact count of failures *among retained commits* is not the failure rate *among all attempts*. If the reference judgments are produced by the same agent being tested, measured agreement may be circular. If scenario assignment correlates with model version or context length, an apparent governance benefit may reflect a confound rather than an architectural effect.

The measurement object may be described abstractly as a retained event history R, a declared measurement procedure M, and a domain reference standard W. An outcome score can then be represented schematically as **S = M(R, W)**. This is not a universal formula for correctness. It emphasizes that the score depends on both what was retained and what warranted interpretation is supplied. Different valid procedures may measure different constructs; two exact numerical scores need not be comparable merely because they use the same range.

An independent investigation must therefore publish more than a summary value. It should specify the event population, missing-data conditions, operational definitions, reference standards, versioned encodings, and uncertainty around the estimates. Where no relevant observation was retained, the appropriate result is *not observable from this record*, not a zero count of violations.

### The observer is part of the design

An additional complication is that an observational interface may influence the generator. Requiring explicit epistemic tags can change generation, attention, and task performance. A governor may alter which proposals reach the environment. A monitoring process may add latency or opportunities for recovery. These changes are not automatically defects: they may be the intended intervention. But they must be distinguished from the instrument's ability to *observe* behavior.

An explicit record may improve accountability because it makes certain claims inspectable. It may also improve or degrade task behavior because constructing the record changes what the generator is prompted to do. A fair comparison should seek to separate these effects rather than credit every observed improvement to numerical analysis or every degradation to governance overhead.

## 4. Epistemic Attribution Without Claims of Interior Transparency

The epistemic dimension is unusually important because the same proposition may be offered in different ways: as a conjecture, an observation, an inference, a reported source claim, or a proposition asserted as established. These roles matter when the proposition becomes consequential.

A hypothesis-testing process must discriminate among conjecture, test evidence, and outcomes in some functional form. Otherwise, the relation between prediction and test is undefined. A generative model may perform much of this discrimination implicitly; removing an external label does not necessarily remove the internal capacity.

A different question arises when the objective is **independently replayable, claim-level epistemic accountability**. An investigator must know what the generator actually declared at the relevant time. Later explanation is not equivalent evidence. The model can compose a convincing retrospective narrative that fails to match its original posture.

The general requirement is a retained representation that makes consequential epistemic distinctions externally inspectable. OTTC instantiates this through a closed EPISTEMIC_TAG vocabulary at its proposal boundary. Other systems might use typed claim graphs, evidence-status relations, or structured assertion records. The representation need not share literal tokens, but the observer must be able to determine the relevant distinctions from records created at the time.

This is an accountability requirement, not a claim that the observer has access to the model's complete private reasoning. A declared inference may have resulted from hidden heuristics, retrieval effects, or misleading context. Its declaration is an observable artifact, not a guaranteed causal account of its production.

Four features must also remain distinct: the generator's declared epistemic posture, the provenance of supporting material, the authority to disclose or admit that material, and the material's evidentiary warrant. A correctly authenticated tool return can be irrelevant or false. A supported inference is not necessarily a direct perception. A committed institutional fact is not immune to subsequent correction.

These distinctions support more informative failure classification. A proposal may violate an operation rule, lack sufficient evidence for its claimed standing, or be empirically contradicted by an appropriate independent test. The first is constitutional invalidity. The second is insufficient warrant. Only the third is empirical refutation of the relevant proposition. A generic verifier FAIL cannot be assumed to mean all three.

### Why the tempting ablation is not enough

A natural experiment deletes an explicit epistemic tag and asks whether the system can still reason and correct itself. It can test the performance cost of removing that interface, but it does not establish whether epistemic reasoning remains elsewhere in the model. Nor does a successful final answer prove that an independent claim-level history remains available.

An intervention that tries to eliminate all epistemic discrimination while still demanding genuine hypothesis testing is different. It has removed a conceptual prerequisite of the required activity. Failure then follows from the intervention's definition rather than providing a useful estimate of architectural performance.

The empirically informative comparisons concern **alternative observable representations**, the fidelity with which they capture consequential claim roles, their effect on behavior, and the reliability of subsequent independent auditing. A successful alternative representation can challenge the necessity or superiority of OTTC's *particular implementation* without refuting the requirement that relevant distinctions be observable when the audit objective demands them.

This separation prevents a research protocol from becoming self-sealing. Logical prerequisites describe what the specified task requires; experiments determine whether proposed architectures satisfy those requirements and whether their additional empirical advantages actually occur.

## 5. The Bidirectional Boundary as a Measurement Surface

The Mutation Boundary Problem is bidirectional, though its two directions have different mechanisms.

On **egress**, generated possibilities become records, commitments, tool actions, or other consequences. The relevant observations include proposals, authorization decisions, state deltas, verification outcomes, and downstream effects.

On **ingress**, external evidence, source claims, instructions, and evaluative framing enter the system and may shape its operative posture before any explicit consequential proposal is emitted. The relevant observations include what material was presented, how it was framed, where it came from, which parts were admitted as evidence, and under what role or authority they were supplied.

The symmetry is not identity. Egress can often be evaluated against an explicit writer or actuation transaction. Ingress may alter a model's later distribution of interpretations without producing a separately identifiable internal state write. An intake gate can record and control source presentation, but it cannot by that fact expose every hidden effect of the input.

Consider two experimental conditions assessing an identical document. In one, the document is presented neutrally. In the other, the same document is accompanied by an unsupported statement that its author is incompetent or deceptive. Both conditions may subsequently produce well-formed epistemic declarations. If the second condition yields systematically more hostile judgments, investigators may have evidence of framing susceptibility. The declared tags alone would not establish that causal effect; the randomized or carefully matched framing intervention would be essential.

An ingress study should distinguish legitimate contextual information from contamination. A user's assertion may be evidence of the user's position even when it is not evidence that the underlying allegation is true. The domain's task and evidentiary rules determine whether and how the assertion should influence the result.

Tool-mediated investigation joins ingress and egress into a single trace. A model requests a source or external action; an authority adjudicates the request; an executor performs it if authorized; the response returns with provenance; an intake mechanism decides what may be disclosed and admitted; the model interprets the result; and a new proposal may become consequential. A tool request is not proof of execution; an executed return is not proof that every statement in the source is true; a subsequent claim is not automatically warranted.

A longitudinal instrument should preserve these as distinct linkable events whenever the research question depends on them. Collapsing them into a single "tool used successfully" flag can conceal source misattribution, unauthorized access, unsupported evidence promotion, or failure to update after contradictory returns.

### Observation does not by itself identify influence

The same caution applies to cross-agent and cross-domain communication. A receiving model may inherit assumptions from material passed by another agent or institution. A reliable record can show what was disclosed, translated, and admitted, but determining why a later inference changed may require interventions that hold the substantive information fixed while varying source, framing, translation, or admission policy.

The methodological implication is that the experiment must record **exposure conditions** as well as **boundary outcomes**. Neither alone is sufficient for every causal question.

## 6. Longitudinal Constructs and Candidate Measures

A general evaluation should not collapse all mutation-boundary behavior into a single scalar. The relevant constructs are related but distinct, and the domain may assign different importance to each.

| Construct | Candidate operational measure | Interpretation risk |
|---|---|---|
| Lawful boundary compliance | Proportion of attempted consequential operations correctly authorized or refused against independently specified rules | An all-reject governor may look safe without accepting required work |
| Epistemic promotion integrity | Unsupported promotions to stronger claimed standing, counted at both proposal and commit levels | A clean committed state can hide frequent improper attempts |
| Evidence-lineage fidelity | Proportion of consequential claims with resolvable, authorized, materially relevant sources | A citation may resolve without supporting the claim |
| Claim-level attribution | Ability of blinded independent reviewers or deterministic validators to reconstruct the original declared claim role | Reconstruction may be impossible when contemporaneous status was not retained |
| Correction fidelity | Proportion of warranted corrections made to the appropriate hypothesis or state | Several lawful corrections may be acceptable; a reversal may reflect a changing world |
| Correction latency | Time or cycle interval from an independently defined decisive evidence event to the warranted revision | Evidence may be delayed, contested, or insufficiently decisive |
| Wrongful suppression | Rate at which proposals required or permitted under specified law are unjustifiably refused | A refusal may be lawful if an evidentiary gate is not satisfied |
| Longitudinal biased governed drift | Evidence-unwarranted directional departure of committed trajectories from domain-grounded reference standards | Motion is not necessarily drift; a flat trajectory may be wrongful stagnation |
| Tool-intent and actuation integrity | Match between requested, authorized, executed, returned, and recorded tool events | A recorded call can be authentic while the source is inaccurate |
| Replay and record integrity | Reproduction of dispositions and state evolution from specified versioned inputs and rules | Deterministic replay can reproduce a consistently wrong law |
| Governance burden | Computation, storage, latency, review effort, and recovery cost per relevant outcome | Cost per step may obscure savings from prevented failure |

These measures should be reported separately before any composite index is considered. A composite may be justified for a particular decision context, but its weights must be explained and sensitivity-tested. Improving one construct cannot be allowed to conceal deterioration in another simply through an opaque aggregate.

### The denominator matters

Violation rates need a well-defined population. If a system logs only accepted writes, the observed rate of unsuccessful factual promotions is censored. An all-reject governor may produce zero committed violations while rejecting every warranted change. A generator that issues fewer proposals may appear safer merely because its opportunities to err were reduced. Rates should therefore specify the denominator: attempted proposals, adjudicated proposals, actual commits, relevant opportunity events, or independent test cases.

Some outcomes must be measured against **opportunities**, not only observed attempts. To identify failures of lawful responsiveness, evaluators need scenarios in which a correction was warranted even if no proposal was generated. An instrument that sees only submitted proposals cannot by itself count omissions. The experimental reference must supply the missing expected-action events.

### Temporal semantics matter

Time can be measured in cycles, tool transactions, wall-clock delay, or domain-specific episodes. These are not interchangeable. A three-cycle correction lag may represent seconds in one system and weeks in another. The relevant clock must be declared in advance.

Likewise, a model's initial claim may be correct when made and later become outdated because the world changed. A newer contrary observation is not automatically falsification of the historical claim. The measurement must distinguish new evidence that invalidates an earlier inference from events that legitimately alter the underlying conditions.

### Biased governed drift requires a reference, not merely a distance

The proposed biased-governed-drift construct merits particular care. A simple distance between initial and final state cannot establish unjustified development. A character's trust may lawfully decline after repeated betrayal; a scientific assessment may lawfully strengthen as independent evidence accumulates; a policy stance may lawfully change after a new authorization or regulation.

One possible study would compare an observed trajectory with a domain-authorized set of admissible trajectories conditioned on the evidence sequence. Deviations could then be characterized by direction, duration, magnitude where meaningful, and relation to specific evidence events. But the reference set itself is a research object requiring expert specification and reliability checks.

No generic ordinal distance or semantic embedding score can substitute for those judgments. And nothing about measuring biased governed drift makes the value a physical entropy-production rate. Thermodynamic correspondence would require a separately justified mapping and empirical demonstration.

## 7. A Comparative Experimental Protocol

A serious research program should compare architectures according to their functions and observed outcomes, not merely whether they adopt a named vocabulary.

The following protocol is a proposed starting design, not a report of completed results.

### 7.1 Define domains and authority first

Select one or more domains with explicit durable state, admissible evidence classes, and consequential update rules. At least one domain should permit relatively inexpensive long-horizon trials; at least one should place different demands on epistemic or authorization integrity. Narrative environments, synthetic scientific tasks, and controlled institutional workflows are possible candidates, provided their domain standards are documented.

Domain experts should specify reference conditions before observing model outcomes: which claims are warranted or unwarranted, which evidence changes are decisive, which updates are permitted or required, and which outcomes remain legitimately unresolved. For contested cases, define an admissible outcome set rather than imposing a fictional single ground truth. Human review procedures and disagreements should be recorded.

The model must not be its own sole judge of evidentiary success. If an LLM assists reference construction, its work should be independently adjudicated and versioned.

### 7.2 Compare functional architecture families

A useful battery could include four families, with deployment feasibility and risk determining which conditions can be ethically run:

1. **Lightly structured baseline:** comparable generator and task environment, without a substantial explicit mutation-governance interface. Use only in a safely sandboxed setting where uncontrolled consequences cannot escape.
2. **Ordinary structured logging and validation:** schema-checked outputs, event recording, or workflow controls without assuming OTTC's specific constitution.
3. **Alternative explicit epistemic-accountability architecture:** structured claim/evidence records or another design that makes consequential epistemic distinctions independently inspectable.
4. **OTTC-shaped architecture:** qualified canonical proposal surface, typed epistemic declarations, separate authority, audit, verification, and—where implemented—downstream Semantic Abacus measurements.

These are functional descriptions, not a ranking. The experiment should record their actual enforcement capabilities rather than infer them from labels. A system that lacks external state authority should not be represented as equivalent to one that has it. Comparisons should distinguish the effect of the **generator**, the **governance arrangement**, and the **measurement interface**.

Where possible, use crossed or staged interventions: the same generator under multiple boundary arrangements, and multiple generators under comparable boundary functions. This will not eliminate every confound—different interfaces can change model behavior—but it makes the source of differences more interpretable.

### 7.3 Construct matched scenario histories

Scenario batteries should contain opportunities for both lawful and unlawful mutation. They should include ambiguous evidence, unsupported certainty, legitimate required updates, conflicting claims, delayed counterevidence, changed external conditions, and tool interactions whose results differ from the original expectation.

Ingress conditions should vary neutral, favorable, hostile, and misleading evaluative framing while the underlying content remains controlled. Egress conditions should include competing proposals, failed authorizations, evidence promotions, lawful commits, and unsuccessful tool attempts.

The horizon must be long enough to test cumulative effects rather than just one or two transitions. Use several horizon lengths, not a single arbitrary maximum. Include context pressure, model-role changes, and recovery conditions where appropriate. Separate a fresh pre-build qualification of a candidate role from regression of a completed system and from longitudinal performance of that system. These answer different questions.

### 7.4 Capture common outputs without erasing architecture differences

For comparison, define a **research-level event ontology** that maps each architecture's records into common conceptual dimensions: proposed claim, evidence event, authority decision, actual consequential change, reference outcome, and time. The mapping must be explicit and versioned.

Do not force a competing architecture to emit OTTC's literal EPISTEMIC_TAG field merely to earn a score. If it offers an equivalent explicit claim taxonomy, evaluate whether the original declaration can be independently reconstructed with comparable fidelity. If it lacks a contemporaneous equivalent, mark the claim-role history as unobservable rather than inventing a status from later prose.

Preserve the native logs alongside any normalized comparison table. Cross-system translation may discard meaning; that risk must remain visible. Independent reviewers should inspect representative cases for translation fidelity.

### 7.5 Predefine outcomes and estimation

Before comparing systems, define primary and secondary outcomes. A sensible primary pair is **unjustified consequential mutation** and **failure of lawful responsiveness**. Other measures—attribution, source linkage, correction latency, record completeness, costs—can explain the pattern.

Use repeated independent runs and scenarios, not one favorable trace. Record model and environment versions, prompt/interface versions, randomization, evidence timing, reference standards, and all exclusions. Where possible, conceal system identity from outcome adjudicators.

Analyze proportions with appropriate denominators and uncertainty intervals. Treat repeated cycles from the same trajectory as dependent observations; hundreds of cycles in one trace are not hundreds of independent experimental units. Estimate results across independent trajectories and scenario families, and report variation across models, domains, languages, and context loads instead of claiming substrate neutrality from pooled averages.

A preregistered statistical plan, including effect sizes and uncertainty, is preferable to selecting whichever metric improves after seeing the result. In settings with qualitative expert judgments, report inter-rater disagreement and adjudication rules. Sensitivity analyses should examine how conclusions change under alternative legitimate reference standards.

### 7.6 Audit the measurement pipeline itself

Plant known faults into some scenarios: a missing proposal, a fabricated tool-return narrative, a stale source, a wrong entity link, an unsupported fact promotion, a wrongful refusal, and a verification report that conflicts with committed state. Test whether the instrument detects the fault or whether the derived metric changes as expected.

Independently replay selected histories where replay is possible. A metric's deterministic reproducibility is necessary for some claims, but its **construct validity** requires more: the reported quantity must discriminate the intended failure from plausible alternatives.

If a measure cannot distinguish unjustified change from warranted development under controlled cases, it has failed its proposed diagnostic purpose even if its calculations are perfectly reproducible. A measure that rewards zero state movement in required-to-update cases has failed for the same reason.

## 8. A Comparative Example: The Same Outcome, Different Evidence

Consider a controlled agent asked to maintain a durable assessment of a supplier over twenty review cycles. The domain's rules require it to record material source evidence and revise the standing assessment when specified thresholds are met. The evaluator supplies one sequence of genuine performance reports, one misleading rumor, one corrected source identification, and a later independently warranted improvement.

Four agents end with the same final supplier assessment. Conventional endpoint accuracy would rate them similarly.

Their histories might nevertheless differ substantially.

One agent proposed the right revisions for well-supported reasons and received lawful commitments. A second repeatedly tried to treat the rumor as established but was consistently stopped by governance. A third ignored the rumor but also failed to propose the required later upgrade, leaving the final state correct only because an authorized human intervened. A fourth used an apparently valid source whose entity identifier belonged to a different supplier, and reached the correct endpoint accidentally.

The final result does not distinguish these mechanisms.

A complete and credible history might. It could reveal the original declared standing of the rumor, the attempted promotions, the independent human intervention, the missing proposal in the third system, and the mistaken source association in the fourth.

But only if those facts were observable. If source-to-claim linkage was never retained, source misattribution may be unmeasurable from the record. If the researcher did not independently establish which updates were warranted, wrongful suppression may remain indistinguishable from legitimate caution.

This example shows why outcome evaluation and boundary evaluation are complements rather than substitutes. A system can achieve an accurate final answer through poor governance. A system can exhibit strong governance while its generator remains error-prone. A compliant boundary may still enforce the wrong domain rule if the ontology was misspecified.

The investigation should therefore report different questions separately: Did the system reach the correct end state? Were the attempted consequential claims epistemically appropriate? Were authorities exercised lawfully? Did the system respond correctly to evidence? Can independent investigators reproduce those judgments from the retained records?

The goal is not to favor the architecture that generates the most elaborate log. It is to determine which systems enable the relevant questions to be answered accurately and which deliver better consequences under the same legitimate requirements.

## 9. Candidate Instruments, Cross-Domain Limits, and Institutional Costs

The Semantic Abacus is one candidate instrument within this methodological space. Its OTTC-specific design begins with canonical semantic tokens and contemporaneous epistemic declarations. Governance resolves consequential proposals; a downstream deterministic layer encodes and compares the retained event history. The companion bridge, *The Semantic Abacus: From Governed Meaning to Longitudinal Measurement*, explains that division of labor through a worked example.

The general measurement argument does not depend on the Abacus's literal token vocabulary. A typed event-sourcing architecture might preserve equivalent proposal and disposition histories. A claim-and-evidence graph might provide an explicit contemporaneous structure for inferential support. A scientific workflow might combine provenance records, hypothesis registries, and independent validation. These alternatives should be judged by the actual information they preserve, the authority controlling it, the reliability of measurement, and their effect on consequential outcomes.

No one representation should be called universal without cross-domain evidence. A narrative system's admissible character development need not admit the same numerical model as a scientific hypothesis update or a legal determination. In some domains the standard can be automated; in others the required distinctions may depend on human expertise, institutional procedure, or legitimately contested judgments.

Federation introduces additional problems. Separate domains may apply different evidentiary standards to related claims. An admissible source-side statement may require translation, disclosure authorization, and receiving-side evaluation before becoming a consequential input elsewhere. An instrument that collapses these steps into a single shared score risks erasing the distinct authorities whose actions it is supposed to measure. Cross-domain measurement may therefore require negotiated mappings, domain-local metrics, and explicit limits on aggregated comparison.

The cost of observation also matters. Retaining event histories consumes storage and computation. Versioned schemas, independent verification, source authorization, and human adjudication impose latency and labor. These costs should be measured alongside avoided failure and recovery costs. A more elaborate governor may be cheaper overall by preventing costly mistakes, or more expensive without material benefit; neither outcome follows from constitutional theory alone.

High-integrity records may also contain confidential, privileged, or sensitive information. A researcher's desire for completeness is not independent authority to retain or disclose everything. Governance of the measurement system includes collection minimization, access controls, audit scope, retention periods, and lawful disclosure. Where privacy constraints prevent full reconstruction, report the resulting limits on inference.

A further methodological danger is *measurement capture*. An organization might optimize the quantities easiest to count—few rejected proposals, little state movement, high formal audit coverage—rather than the substantive goals they were intended to approximate. External validation, multiple measures, and adversarial tests are needed to avoid mistaking favorable indicators for adequate governance.

## 10. Falsification, Limits, and Research Priorities

The measurement problem should not be turned into an argument that its preferred instruments cannot fail.

Several distinct claims are open to challenge.

**Observational adequacy claims** fail if records advertised as sufficient cannot support independent reconstruction of the relevant events, or if a cheaper alternative preserves equally reliable information. The need for an observable property follows from the defined audit question; the superiority of a particular recording architecture does not.

**Metric validity claims** fail if a measure does not distinguish its proposed construct from confounding alternatives. A drift metric that labels lawful development as deterioration, or rewards wrongful refusal as stability, lacks the validity claimed for it. An epistemic attribution score based entirely on reconstructed statuses may not measure contemporaneous declarations.

**Architecture-performance claims** fail or require qualification if controlled trials show no improvement, deterioration, or unacceptable costs relative to appropriate alternatives. OTTC's proposed advantages, including those of the Semantic Abacus interface, belong in this category. A successful alternative explicit representation may defeat a uniqueness claim without demonstrating that independently auditable epistemology can be purely implicit.

**Causal claims** fail if matched interventions do not support the hypothesized relationship, or if the association disappears under control for model quality, scenario variation, exposure, or operator behavior.

Several research priorities follow. First, construct small, independently adjudicated benchmark histories containing both wrongful commits and wrongful refusals, including cases where the world changes rather than an earlier claim being false. Second, compare the fidelity of different explicit epistemic records and identify what each makes unobservable. Third, test whether matched long-horizon trials reveal failures not predicted by per-cycle governance metrics. Fourth, study ingress effects using controlled framing and source-provenance conditions. Fifth, measure the costs and reliability of progressively externalized authority and instrumentation. Sixth, evaluate whether proposed semantic-to-numeric encodings remain stable and meaningful across domains, languages, and versions.

The broader program should remain receptive to contrary evidence. Some tasks may not require elaborate claim-level history. Certain kinds of internal access may provide additional diagnostic information that changes the appropriate measurement design. Stronger event records may not improve the generator's reasoning, or may do so only under some model and domain conditions. A different governance architecture may produce better lawful outcomes or more economical observation than OTTC.

None of those possibilities dissolves the original Mutation Boundary Problem. They refine how it should be studied.

The program also must avoid promoting analogy into physical measurement without further evidence. Governing transitions consumes real computational and organizational resources, and studying those costs is legitimate. But a numerical index of semantic drift is not a thermodynamic entropy-production rate merely because the phenomena admit suggestive analogies. Physical correspondences require separately specified quantities and empirical tests.

## 11. Conclusion: From Constitutional Intention to Independent Evidence

The Mutation Boundary Problem asks how opaque generative possibilities can acquire durable consequence under intelligible and legitimate conditions. Governance architectures propose answers: boundaries, authority allocations, evidentiary rules, and permitted transitions.

The measurement problem asks a further question: **how could investigators know whether those answers actually work across time?**

A constitutional specification can describe what should happen. A local validator can check whether one transaction followed specified rules. An end-state benchmark can show whether a task concluded successfully. None alone establishes that the intervening history preserved epistemic distinctions, responded appropriately to evidence, and avoided cumulative unjustified change.

A credible longitudinal study needs explicit claims about what can be observed, how the events are linked, which quantities can be measured, what independent standards make the quantities meaningful, and which interventions can support causal conclusions. It must distinguish accepted consequences from rejected attempts, recorded sources from warranted assertions, factual contradiction from rule violation, and adaptive change from drift or stagnation.

These distinctions do not require complete visibility into a model's interior. They require humility about the interface's limits. The declared epistemic behavior of a generator can be an important object of study without being mistaken for its complete hidden cognition. Deterministic encoding can make that history reproducibly countable without turning numbers into truth. Independent governance can preserve authoritative records without rendering every external source infallible.

OTTC's Semantic Abacus offers one proposed implementation of the semantic-to-numeric step. Other designs may satisfy the observational and measurement requirements differently or better. The task of research is to identify which measures are valid, which architectures actually improve consequential behavior, and where their costs and limitations lie.

The central shift is from **claiming that a system is governed** to **making the adequacy of that governance independently testable**. The relevant evidence is neither the generator's confidence nor the elegance of the constitution, but the record of what was proposed, admitted, refused, committed, contradicted, corrected, and sustained under appropriate standards.

That is how the Mutation Boundary Problem becomes not only a question of architectural legitimacy but a subject of empirical investigation.
